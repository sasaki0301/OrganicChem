Acids and Bases: Three Models

Learn to recognize proton transfer, trace conjugate acid–base pairs, and use electron pairs to identify Lewis acids and bases. These ideas prepare you to study organic acid–base reactions.

Suggested time: 50–60 minutes. Work each example on paper before revealing an answer. Practice on this page is not scored or recorded.

1. Three useful models

ModelAcidBaseMain context
ArrheniusIncreases H₃O⁺ in waterIncreases OH⁻ in waterAqueous solutions
Brønsted–LowryDonates H⁺Accepts H⁺Proton transfer; water is not required
LewisAccepts an electron pairDonates an electron pairElectron-pair donation; no proton transfer is required

These models emphasize different features. A single reaction can fit more than one description.

Worked example 1 · In water

HCl and NaOH

HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻

HCl increases hydronium concentration in water and acts as an Arrhenius acid. We show H₃O⁺ because aqueous protons are associated with water.

NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻   (in water)

Dissolved NaOH supplies hydroxide ions, so it is an Arrhenius base.

What about NH₃? Ammonia contains no OH⁻ in its formula, but it reacts with water to increase OH⁻ concentration: NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻. Under the broader “increases OH⁻ in water” wording it is an Arrhenius base. Some introductory texts use a narrower definition focused on direct OH⁻ release; either way, NH₃ is clearly a weak Brønsted base.

2. Brønsted–Lowry acids, bases, and conjugate pairs

A proton is H⁺. In a Brønsted–Lowry reaction, follow the H⁺: the acid donates it and the base accepts it. A conjugate acid–base pair consists of two species that differ by one H⁺.

Worked example 2 · Track the proton

Ammonia and water

NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻

  1. NH₃ becomes NH₄⁺: it gains H⁺, so NH₃ is the base and NH₄⁺ is its conjugate acid.
  2. H₂O becomes OH⁻: it loses H⁺, so H₂O is the acid and OH⁻ is its conjugate base.
  3. Check both pairs: NH₃/NH₄⁺ and H₂O/OH⁻ each differ by one H⁺.

These roles refer to the forward reaction. In the reverse direction, the conjugate acid can donate H⁺ and the conjugate base can accept it.

Quick check 1 · Pair matching

In HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻, identify the two conjugate pairs.

Show answer and explanation
HCl/Cl⁻ and H₂O/H₃O⁺. HCl donates H⁺ to water; the species in each pair differ by one proton.

3. Lewis acids and bases: follow an electron pair

A Lewis base donates an electron pair to form a bond; a Lewis acid accepts the pair. A proton is a Lewis acid because it can accept an electron pair.

Worked example 3 · No proton transfer

Ammonia and boron trifluoride

BF₃ + :NH₃ → F₃B←NH₃

NH₃ donates a nitrogen lone pair and is the Lewis base. Electron-deficient BF₃ accepts that pair at boron and is the Lewis acid. The arrow here shows the pair’s donor and acceptor in forming the B–N bond.

Because no proton is transferred in this example, it is a Lewis acid–base reaction but not a Brønsted–Lowry proton-transfer reaction.

Worked example 4 · Both models

Ammonia accepts a proton from hydronium

NH₃ + H₃O⁺ → NH₄⁺ + H₂O

Brønsted–Lowry: H₃O⁺ donates H⁺ and NH₃ accepts it. Lewis: N donates a lone pair to H⁺, which accepts the pair. Draw a curved arrow from the N lone pair to H and another from the broken O–H bond to O.

4. Strong, weak, concentrated, and dilute

Strength concerns the extent of reaction with water at equilibrium under specified conditions. A strong acid transfers its proton to water essentially completely in the usual dilute aqueous model; a weak acid reacts only partially. A weak base also reacts partially with water.

Examples in water

HCl is a strong acid; CH₃COOH (acetic acid) is weak. NaOH supplies OH⁻ essentially completely upon dissolution; NH₃ is a weak base in water.

Ca(OH)₂ has limited solubility, but the portion that dissolves dissociates into ions. Solubility and base strength are distinct.

Concentration is different

Concentration is how much solute is present per volume. A strong acid can be dilute, and a weak acid can be concentrated.

Do not infer an acid’s intrinsic strength from one pH measurement without knowing concentration and conditions.

Worked example 5 · Acetic acid

Identify the conjugate pairs

CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺

Acetic acid donates H⁺ to water. The pairs are CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻ and H₂O/H₃O⁺. The equilibrium lies mostly toward the undissociated acid in the usual dilute aqueous case; some ions are still present.

Direction: For proton-transfer equilibria, compare the acid and base on each side. Equilibrium tends to favor the side containing the weaker acid and weaker base, subject to the conditions and relative amounts.

5. Amphiprotic species: the role depends on the reaction

An amphiprotic species can donate or accept H⁺ in different reactions. Water and bicarbonate are examples. The broader term amphoteric means able to act as an acid or base; amphiprotic specifically concerns proton transfer.

Water as a base

HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻

H₂O accepts H⁺ and becomes H₃O⁺.

Water as an acid

NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻

H₂O donates H⁺ and becomes OH⁻. NH₃ is a weak base in water.

Worked example 6 · Bicarbonate

Same species, different roles

HCO₃⁻ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ + OH⁻

HCO₃⁻ accepts H⁺ and acts as the base; H₂O donates H⁺ and acts as the acid.

HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻ ⇌ CO₃²⁻ + H₂O

HCO₃⁻ donates H⁺ and acts as the acid; OH⁻ accepts H⁺ and acts as the base. Balance both atoms and charge when checking the equations.

6. pH: compare aqueous solutions

In dilute aqueous solutions, an introductory approximation is pH ≈ −log₁₀[H₃O⁺], using molar concentration. Strictly, pH is defined using hydronium activity. At 25 °C, neutral water has pH 7; aqueous solutions with pH below 7 are acidic and those above 7 are basic. Neutral pH changes with temperature.

Worked example 7 · A logarithmic comparison

pH 3 versus pH 6

The difference is 6 − 3 = 3 pH units. A difference of one unit corresponds to a factor of 10 in hydronium activity; for dilute solutions using the concentration approximation, the pH 3 solution has approximately 10³ = 1,000 times the hydronium concentration of the pH 6 solution.

This compares the acidity of the solutions; it does not by itself tell us which acid is intrinsically stronger.

Quick check 2 · pH difference

For two dilute aqueous solutions, how does [H₃O⁺] at pH 4 compare with that at pH 6?

Show answer and explanation
The difference is 2 units, so the pH 4 solution has approximately 10² = 100 times the hydronium concentration.

Challenge · Trace both pairs

In HSO₄⁻ + H₂O ⇌ SO₄²⁻ + H₃O⁺, identify the forward acid and base and both conjugate pairs.

Show answer and explanation
HSO₄⁻ is the acid; H₂O is the base. Their conjugate pairs are HSO₄⁻/SO₄²⁻ and H₂O/H₃O⁺. The proton lost by HSO₄⁻ is gained by H₂O.

8. Key takeaways

  • Arrhenius: increases hydronium or hydroxide in water.
  • Brønsted–Lowry: acid donates H⁺; base accepts H⁺. Conjugate partners differ by one H⁺.
  • Lewis: acid accepts an electron pair; base donates one.
  • Strength and concentration describe different properties. NH₃ is a weak base in water.
  • At 25 °C, neutral aqueous solutions have pH 7; each pH unit represents a factor of ten in hydronium activity.

Before the quiz, trace H⁺ in an equation, label both conjugate pairs, and explain which species donates an electron pair.
