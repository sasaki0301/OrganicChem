Polar Covalent Bonds and Electronegativity

Why does oxygen attract bonding electrons more strongly than hydrogen? Learn how this difference helps explain bond polarity, molecular polarity, and interactions with water.

Suggested time: 40–50 minutes. Try the questions before checking each explanation. Practice on this page does not record a score.

What you will learn

  • Describe electronegativity and use general periodic trends.
  • Compare electronegativity values and assign partial charges in simple bonds.
  • Use shape and bond dipoles to predict whether a simple molecule has a net dipole.
  • Relate polarity and intermolecular interactions to examples of solubility.
Recall: A covalent bond involves electron density shared between atoms. A polar bond has an uneven distribution of that shared density; the atoms do not become full ions simply because partial charges appear.

1. Electronegativity: attraction in a bond

Electronegativity (EN) describes how strongly an atom attracts shared electrons in a chemical bond. In general, EN increases from left to right across a period and decreases down a group. There are exceptions, so use tabulated values when needed. Fluorine is the most electronegative element.

Worked example 1 · Periodic trend

Oxygen or sulfur?

O and S are in the same group. O is above S and is more electronegative. Its bonding electrons are generally attracted more strongly.

Quick check 1

Between nitrogen and oxygen, which is more electronegative according to the table?

Show answer and explanation
Oxygen. O has EN 3.44 and N has EN 3.04; oxygen attracts shared electron density more strongly in this comparison.

2. From electronegativity to bond polarity

For a bond between atoms A and B, calculate the absolute difference:

ΔEN = |EN(A) − EN(B)|

A bond between identical atoms has equal attraction in this simple model. As the difference increases, shared electron density is drawn more strongly toward the more electronegative atom. Bonding lies on a continuum; numerical cutoffs are rough conventions and vary by source.

BondΔEN from this tableInterpretation
H–H0.00Nonpolar covalent: identical atoms.
C–H0.35Weakly polar; often treated as approximately nonpolar in introductory organic chemistry.
H–Cl0.96Polar covalent; electron density is drawn toward Cl.
O–H1.24Polar covalent; electron density is drawn toward O.
Worked example 2 · Calculate and interpret

Analyze a C–O bond

  1. Look up: C = 2.55 and O = 3.44.
  2. Subtract: ΔEN = |3.44 − 2.55| = 0.89.
  3. Compare: O is more electronegative, so the C–O bond is polar covalent and electron density is shifted toward O.
  4. Label: in this bond, C is δ+ and O is δ−.

Cδ+ → Oδ−

Here the arrow points toward the electron-rich end. The δ symbols mean partial charges, not the full charges of ions. The displayed arrow is a teaching shorthand for the chemistry dipole-arrow convention.

Do not apply a cutoff mechanically: EN differences are useful estimates. The bond environment and other evidence matter, especially for borderline cases and comparisons with ionic substances.

Quick check 2

For N–H, which atom is δ−? Calculate ΔEN using the table.

Show answer and explanation
N is δ− because 3.04 > 2.20. ΔEN = |3.04 − 2.20| = 0.84; the N–H bond is polar covalent.

3. Bond polarity is not the whole molecule

A bond dipole describes charge separation in a particular bond. A molecular dipole is the vector result for the whole molecule. Shape, the directions and magnitudes of bond dipoles, and lone-pair electron density all matter.

Worked example 3 · Compare shapes

CO₂ and H₂O

Both molecules have polar bonds. CO₂ is linear with identical C–O bond dipoles pointing in opposite directions, so they cancel. H₂O is bent; its O–H bond dipoles do not cancel, and the molecule has a net dipole.

A practical prediction sequence

  1. Draw a reasonable Lewis structure and identify the molecular shape.
  2. Identify polar bonds and the more electronegative end of each.
  3. Consider the three-dimensional arrangement and whether dipoles cancel. Include relevant lone pairs in the reasoning.
  4. State whether the molecule has a net dipole; use symmetry only after checking that the surrounding atoms and bond dipoles are comparable.

Quick check 3 · Ammonia

NH₃ has three N–H bonds and one lone pair on N. Is it polar or nonpolar?

Show answer and explanation
Polar. NH₃ is trigonal pyramidal, not flat. Its bond dipoles do not cancel, giving a net dipole toward the nitrogen side.
Common mistake: A molecule can have polar bonds and still be nonpolar overall. Symmetry can lead to cancellation when the bond dipoles balance; merely counting polar bonds is not enough.

4. Polarity in organic molecules

Ethanol and water

Ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH, has polar O–H and C–O bonds. Its –OH group can form hydrogen bonds with water. Ethanol and water are miscible.

Hexane and water

Hexane, C₆H₁₄, is a nonpolar hydrocarbon. It cannot hydrogen-bond with water and has very low solubility in water. Hexane molecules still attract water through dispersion forces, but these interactions do not make the liquids mix well.

“Like dissolves like” is a useful starting guide, not a guarantee. Solubility depends on the balance of intermolecular interactions, molecular size and structure, and temperature. A polar group does not guarantee high water solubility if a large nonpolar region dominates.

Worked example 4 · Predict a trend

Compare an alcohol with a longer carbon chain

Both ethanol and 1-hexanol have an –OH group, but 1-hexanol has a much larger nonpolar carbon chain and is much less soluble in water. The whole structure matters alongside the polar group.

Polarity can also influence boiling points and reaction behavior. It is one factor among several, including molecular size, shape, and the types of intermolecular forces present.

Challenge · Compare two molecules

CH₃Cl has polar C–Cl and nearly nonpolar C–H bonds. CCl₄ has four equivalent C–Cl bonds in a tetrahedral arrangement. Predict which molecule has a net dipole and explain.

Show answer and explanation
CH₃Cl is polar; CCl₄ is nonpolar. CH₃Cl's different surrounding atoms give an unbalanced distribution. In tetrahedral CCl₄, the four equivalent C–Cl bond dipoles cancel by symmetry.

6. Summary and key terms

  • Electronegativity: attraction for shared electron density in a bond.
  • Bond polarity: uneven electron distribution; the more electronegative end is generally δ−.
  • Molecular polarity: net charge separation determined by the entire molecule and its geometry.
  • Hydrogen bonding: an intermolecular attraction relevant when ethanol interacts with water.
  • Polarity is a useful tool for explaining solubility and reactivity, but whole-molecule structure matters.
