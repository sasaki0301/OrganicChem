Formal Charges

Learn to account for electrons in a Lewis structure, identify formal charges, and check that the charges add up to the charge of the molecule or ion.

Suggested time: 45–55 minutes. Draw the structures and try the calculations before revealing the solutions. Practice does not record a score.

What you will learn

  • Calculate an atom’s formal charge using valence, nonbonding, and bonding electrons.
  • Count single, double, and triple bonds correctly when applying the formula.
  • Check that formal charges add up to the overall charge of a molecule or ion.
  • Use formal charges, electron counts, and octets to compare simple Lewis structures.
  • Recognize positive and negative carbon centers common in organic chemistry.
Connection to Lesson 1: A formal charge is an electron-bookkeeping assignment for a particular Lewis structure. A partial charge (δ+ or δ−) describes uneven electron density in a polar bond. They are related ideas but are not the same quantity.

1. What is formal charge?

Formal charge (FC) is the charge assigned to an atom in a particular Lewis structure when the bonding electrons are divided equally between the bonded atoms. It is a useful model, not a direct measurement of the atom’s actual charge.

FC = V − N − B/2

SymbolMeaningWhat to count
VValence electrons for the neutral, isolated atomC = 4, N = 5, O = 6, H = 1 for these examples.
NNonbonding electrons on the chosen atomCount electrons, not lone pairs: one lone pair = 2 electrons.
BBonding electrons in bonds attached to that atomSingle = 2, double = 4, triple = 6 bonding electrons.

Shortcut for ordinary two-electron bonds: FC = V − nonbonding electrons − bond lines

Here, a single bond counts as one line, a double bond as two, and a triple bond as three. This shortcut is the same calculation as B/2; it is not a second charge added to the first.

Counting example

How many bond lines?

In H–C≡N, carbon has one single bond and one triple bond: 4 bond lines, or 8 bonding electrons. Nitrogen has 3 bond lines to carbon and one lone pair.

Quick check · Count electrons

An oxygen atom has two lone pairs and one double bond. What are N and B?

Show answer and explanation
N = 4 and B = 4. Two lone pairs contain four nonbonding electrons; a double bond contains four bonding electrons.

2. Worked example: oxygen in water

Worked example 1 · H₂O

Calculate the formal charge on oxygen

In the usual Lewis structure, H–O–H, oxygen has two O–H single bonds and two lone pairs.

  1. V: oxygen has 6 valence electrons as a neutral atom.
  2. N: two lone pairs contain 4 electrons.
  3. B: two single bonds contain 4 bonding electrons.
  4. Calculate: FC = 6 − 4 − 4/2 = 0.

Using the shortcut: 6 − 4 − 2 bond lines = 0. Each hydrogen has FC = 1 − 0 − 1 = 0, so the sum is 0, matching neutral H₂O.

Important: Oxygen in H₂O has formal charge 0, even though O–H bonds are polar and oxygen is partially negative (δ−). Formal charge assumes equal division of bonding electrons; polarity considers their uneven distribution.

3. Worked examples: charged molecules

Ammonium, NH₄⁺

The standard Lewis structure has N bonded to four H atoms with no lone pair on N.

For N: V = 5, N = 0, B = 8.

FC = 5 − 0 − 8/2 = +1

Each H has FC = 0. The sum is +1, matching NH₄⁺.

Hydronium, H₃O⁺

The standard Lewis structure has O bonded to three H atoms with one lone pair on O.

For O: V = 6, N = 2, B = 6.

FC = 6 − 2 − 6/2 = +1

Each H has FC = 0. The sum is +1, matching H₃O⁺.

Why the structure matters

Check hydronium’s electron budget

O contributes 6; three H contribute 3; subtract 1 for the + charge. Total = 8 valence electrons. Three O–H bonds use 6 and the lone pair uses 2. Three bonds plus one lone pair gives oxygen an octet. Assigning four O–H bonds would describe a different atom count and is not H₃O⁺.

Quick check · Hydroxide

In OH⁻, O has one O–H single bond and three lone pairs. Find O’s formal charge.

Show answer and explanation
O: V = 6, N = 6, B = 2. FC = 6 − 6 − 2/2 = −1. H is 0, so the total is −1.

Worked example 2 · Multiple bonds

HCN: H–C≡N

H has one bond and no lone pairs: 1 − 0 − 1 = 0. C has four bond lines and no lone pairs: 4 − 0 − 4 = 0. N has a triple bond and one lone pair: 5 − 2 − 3 = 0. Their charges sum to 0, matching neutral HCN.

5. How to use formal charges

  1. Count total valence electrons, adding electrons for negative charge and subtracting for positive charge.
  2. Draw a plausible Lewis structure, checking connectivity, electron count, and appropriate valence shells.
  3. Calculate the formal charge of each atom and compare their sum with the overall charge.
  4. When comparing valid contributors with the same atom arrangement, consider octets and formal charges together. Often contributors with small formal charges and negative charge on a more electronegative atom are more important.
A correct total is necessary but not sufficient. A structure can have the right formal-charge sum and still have the wrong electron count, connectivity, or an invalid valence-shell arrangement. Common octet exceptions and resonance require additional care. Formal charge also offers clues about possible reaction sites; it does not predict reactivity on its own.
Worked example 3 · Check a contributor

Nitrite, NO₂⁻

Consider one contributor [O=N–O]⁻, where N has one lone pair. The double-bonded O has two lone pairs; the single-bonded O has three. FCs are N: 5 − 2 − 3 = 0; double-bonded O: 6 − 4 − 2 = 0; single-bonded O: 6 − 6 − 1 = −1. Total = −1.

A second valid contributor places the double bond and −1 on opposite oxygens. These are Lewis contributors to one resonance hybrid; the formal charges in a single contributor should not be mistaken for a permanently localized charge distribution.

6. Guided and independent practice

Use the shortcut V − nonbonding electrons − bond lines. Select a specified atom, calculate each number, and check your work. This practice is not graded or recorded.

Interactive drill · Build the calculation

Practice A · Ammonia

NH₃ has three N–H single bonds and one lone pair on N. Find the formal charge on N and the overall sum.

Show answer and explanation
N: 5 − 2 − 3 = 0. Each H is 0, so the total is 0.

Practice B · Carbon dioxide

In O=C=O, each O has two lone pairs and C has none. Find the formal charges on C and on each O.

Show answer and explanation
C: 4 − 0 − 4 = 0. Each O: 6 − 4 − 2 = 0. The total is 0.

Practice C · Find the mistake

A student says the O in H₃O⁺ has formal charge +2 because oxygen has four bonds and no lone pairs. Identify two problems.

Show answer and explanation
H₃O⁺ has three O–H bonds and one lone pair on O. Thus O = 6 − 2 − 3 = +1; adding the three zero-charge H atoms gives the ion’s +1 charge.

Challenge · Formal versus partial charge

Why can oxygen in H₂O have formal charge 0 while still carrying δ−?

Show answer and explanation
Formal charge divides bonding electrons equally as bookkeeping. Oxygen attracts the shared electron density more strongly than H, giving a partial negative charge in the real polar bonds.

7. Key takeaways

  • FC = V − N − B/2; count N in electrons, not pairs.
  • For ordinary Lewis bonds, FC = V − nonbonding electrons − bond lines.
  • The sum of atomic formal charges equals the overall molecule or ion charge.
  • A correct sum does not by itself prove the Lewis structure is valid.
  • Formal charge is not the same as a partial charge or measured electron distribution.

Before the quiz, try H₂O, H₃O⁺, NH₄⁺, and CH₃⁻ without referring to the solutions.
