How the ideas connect

Electronegativity helps explain bond polarity. Lewis structures and formal charges account for valence electrons. Resonance contributors help describe delocalization. Those tools help us compare acid–base behavior and reason about organic reactions.

Each model has a purpose and limits: formal charge is bookkeeping for a Lewis structure, while partial charge describes uneven electron density. Use the whole structure and the reaction context when making a prediction.

1. Electronegativity and polarity

  • Electronegativity describes an atom’s attraction for shared electron density in a bond. Greater differences usually mean greater bond polarity; numerical cutoffs are approximate.
  • In O–H, O is more electronegative and is δ−; H is δ+. C–H is weakly polar and often treated as approximately nonpolar in introductory organic chemistry.
  • A molecule’s net dipole depends on bond dipoles and three-dimensional arrangement, not simply on the presence of polar bonds.
Compare two molecules

CO₂: equal C=O bond dipoles point in opposite directions in the linear molecule and cancel. H₂O: the O–H bond dipoles do not cancel in the bent molecule.

Checkpoint · Molecular polarity

Why can CO₂ have polar C=O bonds yet be nonpolar overall?

Show answer and explanation
Its two equivalent bond dipoles point in opposite directions along a linear structure, so their vector sum is zero. Review Lesson 1 if needed.

2. Formal charge

FC = V − N − B/2

V = valence electrons in the neutral atom; N = nonbonding electrons on that atom; B = bonding electrons around it. For ordinary two-electron bonds, use the equivalent shortcut V − nonbonding electrons − bond lines; a double bond counts as two lines.

The sum of atomic formal charges must equal the overall molecular or ionic charge. A correct sum is necessary but does not alone prove the structure is valid: check electron count, connectivity, and appropriate valence too.

Checkpoint · Hydronium

In H₃O⁺, oxygen has three single bonds and one lone pair. What is its formal charge?

Show answer and explanation
O has V = 6, N = 2, and B = 6. FC = 6 − 2 − 6/2 = +1. Each H is 0; the sum is +1, matching the ion. Review Lesson 2 for the electron-counting procedure.

3. Resonance and delocalization

Resonance contributors retain the same atom connectivity, electron count, and overall charge but differ in electron placement. Curved arrows track electron pairs in common Lewis-bookkeeping patterns. The actual species is one resonance hybrid; it does not oscillate between the drawings.

Example · Acetate

CH₃–C(=Oa)–Ob⁻   ↔   CH₃–C(–Oa⁻)=Ob

The subscripts a and b track the same oxygens in both contributors. The two major contributors are equivalent; the hybrid has equivalent C–O bonds and delocalized negative charge. The charge is not located on each O for half the time.

When comparing valid contributors, consider valence and octets, formal-charge magnitudes, and charge location together. Electron-deficient species such as carbocations can be legitimate octet exceptions. The number of drawings alone does not establish a universal stability ranking.

Checkpoint · Valid contributors

Is a drawing that moves a hydrogen atom to a different oxygen a resonance contributor?

Show answer and explanation
No. Resonance contributors retain the atom-to-atom connectivity. Moving an atom changes the bonding arrangement and describes a different structure or process. Review Lesson 3 for electron-pair arrows.

4. Acid–base models and conjugate pairs

ModelAcidBase

Brønsted–LowryDonates H⁺Accepts H⁺

In a proton-transfer reaction the acid and its conjugate base differ by one H⁺, as do the base and its conjugate acid. A Lewis reaction such as BF₃ accepting an NH₃ lone pair does not require proton transfer.

Checkpoint · Trace H⁺

For NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻, identify the acid, base, and both conjugate pairs in the forward direction.

Show answer and explanation
NH₃ accepts H⁺ and is the base; H₂O donates H⁺ and is the acid. The pairs are NH₃/NH₄⁺ and H₂O/OH⁻. NH₃ is a weak base in water. Review Lesson 4 for the three models.
Strength versus concentration: A weak acid can be concentrated and a strong acid can be dilute. At 25 °C, neutral aqueous solutions have pH 7; one pH unit corresponds to a tenfold change in hydronium activity.

5. Organic acids and bases

An acid donates H⁺ to form its conjugate base. A lower pKₐ indicates a stronger acid when values are compared in the same solvent and under comparable conditions. A stronger acid corresponds to a weaker conjugate base in that comparison.

AcidApproximate pKₐConjugate base
Acetic acid4.8Acetate
Phenol10Phenoxide
Ethanol16Ethoxide

Approximate aqueous reference values near 25 °C; very weak acid values may be estimated or extrapolated. Values vary with source, solvent, and conditions.

For these examples, acidity decreases acetic acid > phenol > ethanol. Acetic acid is weak in water even though it is stronger than ethanol. Resonance, induction, hybridization, solvation, and relative stabilization of the acid and conjugate base all contribute; no single factor universally decides every comparison.

Methylamine and aniline are weak bases in water. Under comparable aqueous conditions, methylamine is more basic; delocalization of the aniline nitrogen lone pair into the aromatic ring helps explain the difference. Carbanion basicity depends on structure and conditions.

Checkpoint · Inductive effect

Why is chloroacetic acid more acidic than acetic acid in a comparable aqueous comparison?

Show answer and explanation
Chlorine withdraws electron density through σ bonds and helps stabilize the conjugate base relative to the acid. Review Lesson 5 for resonance, induction, and pKₐ comparisons.

6. Put the chapter together

Integrated example · Acetic acid and hydroxide

CH₃COOH + OH⁻ ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O

  1. Polarity: The O–H bond of acetic acid is polar; oxygen attracts shared electron density more strongly than hydrogen.
  2. Acid–base roles: Acetic acid donates H⁺; hydroxide accepts it. The pairs are CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻ and H₂O/OH⁻.
  3. Formal charge and resonance: Acetate has overall charge −1. Its two equivalent major Lewis contributors place a −1 formal charge on different oxygens; the hybrid delocalizes that charge.
  4. Equilibrium: In the introductory aqueous comparison, water (pKₐ ≈ 15.7) is the weaker acid than acetic acid (pKₐ ≈ 4.8), so proton transfer strongly favors acetate and water.
