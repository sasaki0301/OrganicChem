Resonance Structures

Use Lewis structures to represent electron delocalization, trace valid changes in electron placement, and connect resonance to molecular structure and stability.

Suggested time: 50–60 minutes. Draw the structures on paper, including lone pairs and formal charges, before revealing answers. Practice is not recorded.

What you will learn

  • Distinguish resonance contributors from the real resonance hybrid.

  • Use curved arrows to track electron pairs in nitrite, carboxylate, and allyl systems.
  • Explain how delocalization relates to bond equivalence and stability.
  • Compare contributors using valence, charge separation, and electronegativity.
Recall Lesson 2: Formal charge is assigned for a particular Lewis structure. When comparing contributors, the overall charge stays the same even though formal charges may be assigned to different atoms.

1. What does resonance describe?

Sometimes one Lewis structure cannot represent the electron distribution well. We draw two or more resonance contributors with the same atoms and connectivity but different electron arrangements. Together they help describe one resonance hybrid.

Think carefully

One species, several drawings

The real molecule or ion does not switch back and forth between the drawings. The drawings are models. Electron density in the hybrid may be spread across several atoms; the hybrid can resemble some contributors more than others.

contributor A   ↔   contributor B

The double-headed resonance arrow (↔) connects drawings of the same species. It is not the equilibrium arrow (⇌), which relates distinct species in a reversible process.

Quick check 1

Does a negative charge in one contributor stay on that atom for part of the time and then jump to another?

Show answer and explanation
No. The contributors are alternative electron-bookkeeping descriptions. In the hybrid the relevant electron density may be delocalized; the charge is not hopping between drawings.

2. Rules for valid contributors

  1. Keep the atom skeleton: atom identities and atom-to-atom connectivity remain the same. Do not move H or another atom.
  2. Keep the electron budget and overall charge: the total valence electron count, number of unpaired electrons, and net charge remain unchanged.
  3. Move appropriate electrons: in the common examples here, move a lone pair or π electrons. The σ framework remains fixed.
  4. Check valence: account for all lone pairs, bonds, and formal charges. Second-row atoms such as C, N, O, and F cannot exceed an octet. An incomplete octet can occur in an electron-deficient species, such as a carbocation.
  5. Use curved arrows correctly: a full curved arrow represents a pair of electrons; its tail begins at the electron pair or bond, and its head points to the atom or bond receiving that pair.
Moving atoms changes the structure: a proton transfer or rearrangement is a chemical change between distinct structures, not resonance. A double-headed resonance arrow is different from the curved arrow used to show electron-pair movement on paper.
Common movement patterns

Where can a pair go?

  • A lone pair next to a suitable π system can become part of a π bond, provided the accompanying electron movement keeps valence valid.
  • A π pair can become a lone pair on an adjacent atom.
  • A π pair can shift toward an adjacent empty p orbital, as in the allyl cation.

These are patterns, not permission to draw an isolated arrow that gives an atom too many bonds. Track all arrows, electrons, and formal charges together.

3. Worked example: nitrite, NO₂⁻

Worked example 1 · Two equivalent contributors

Track the double bond and charges

[O=N–O⁻]   ↔   [⁻O–N=O]

Each contributor has overall charge −1. In each drawing, the single-bonded O has three lone pairs and formal charge −1; the double-bonded O has two lone pairs and formal charge 0. N has one lone pair and formal charge 0. Total valence electrons: 5 + 2(6) + 1 = 18.

  1. Start with the left contributor. Draw one full curved arrow from a lone pair on the single-bonded O⁻ toward its N–O bond, forming a π bond.
  2. At the same time, draw a second full curved arrow from the original N=O π bond to the other O, creating a lone pair there.
  3. Recalculate formal charges and check each octet. The result is the right contributor; the atoms and total charge are unchanged.

The two oxygen environments are equivalent in nitrite. The two N–O bonds in the hybrid are equivalent, with bond character intermediate between an idealized single and double bond.

Quick check 2 · Electron counting

Why must the original N=O π pair move when the other O forms a π bond to nitrogen?

Show answer and explanation
If both N–O connections became double bonds while N kept its lone pair, nitrogen would have ten electrons around it. Moving the original π pair to oxygen preserves nitrogen’s octet and the electron budget.

4. Resonance in organic molecules

Worked example 2 · Acetate

Two equivalent carboxylate contributors

CH₃–C(=Oa)–Ob⁻   ↔   CH₃–C(–Oa⁻)=Ob

The subscripts a and b label the same oxygen atoms in both drawings; they are teaching labels, not extra atoms. Both drawings describe acetate, CH₃COO⁻. The singly bonded O⁻ has three lone pairs, and the double-bonded O has two.

  1. Move a lone pair on O⁻ into the adjacent C–O bond.
  2. Move the existing C=O π pair onto the other O.
  3. Check that carbon still has an octet and the overall charge remains −1.

The two major contributors are equivalent. In the hybrid, the two C–O bonds are equivalent and the negative charge is delocalized over the oxygens. This does not mean each oxygen carries −1 for half the time.

Worked example 3 · Allyl cation

Electron pair moves toward an empty orbital

CH₂=CH–CH₂⁺   ↔   ⁺CH₂–CH=CH₂

The three-carbon skeleton remains unchanged. Draw a curved arrow from the π bond toward the bond joining the middle carbon to the positively charged terminal carbon. In the other contributor, the double bond is on the opposite side and the formal positive charge is on the first terminal carbon. The hybrid delocalizes the positive charge across the terminal positions.

Resonance stabilizes an allylic carbocation relative to a closely comparable localized carbocation, but substitution, solvent, and other effects also affect stability. Do not rank every allylic carbocation above every non-allylic carbocation.

Benzene

Two equivalent Kekulé contributors show alternating double bonds in different positions. The six π electrons are delocalized, and the six C–C bonds are equivalent. Benzene’s cyclic conjugation is associated with aromatic stabilization.

Phenoxide

Phenoxide has a contributor with negative charge on oxygen and additional contributors placing formal negative charge at ortho or para ring positions. These contributors help describe electron delocalization; they do not represent separate ions exchanging places.

Comparing stability: Delocalization is one factor that helps stabilize carboxylate ions relative to comparable localized anions. Solvation, inductive effects, molecular structure, and the comparison chosen also matter; the number of drawn contributors alone is not a universal measure of stability.

5. Comparing contributions

Valid contributors may be equivalent or nonequivalent. Equivalent contributors contribute equally by symmetry. For nonequivalent contributors, use these guidelines together:

  • Structures with valid valences and complete octets on second-row atoms are generally important; account for legitimate electron-deficient exceptions.
  • Less unnecessary charge separation often favors a contributor.
  • When other features are comparable, negative formal charge is generally more favorable on a more electronegative atom such as O than on C.

These are guidelines, not independent absolute rules. Consider valence, charge, electronegativity, and the specific system together. The resonance hybrid is the species; no single contributor is a separate rapidly interconverting molecule.

Ozone · Apply the rules

O₃ has two equivalent contributors

⁻O–O⁺=O   ↔   O=O⁺–O⁻

Each contributor has a +1 formal charge on the central O and a −1 on the single-bonded terminal O; the net charge is zero. The two O–O bonds in the hybrid are equivalent and intermediate between idealized single and double bonds.

Practice 4 · Allyl cation

Draw the other contributor for CH₂=CH–CH₂⁺. Where is the positive formal charge in each contributor?

Show answer and explanation
CH₂=CH–CH₂⁺ ↔ ⁺CH₂–CH=CH₂. The positive formal charge is on one terminal carbon in the first contributor and on the other terminal carbon in the second. The atoms remain in the same order.

Challenge · Check an invalid drawing

In acetate a student draws both C–O bonds as double bonds but keeps the C–CH₃ bond. Why is this drawing invalid for the carbon atom?

Show answer and explanation
Carbon would have five bond lines (two + two + one), or ten bonding electrons around it. This exceeds the octet available to second-row carbon. The original C=O π pair must move to oxygen as the other π bond forms.

7. Key takeaways

  • Contributors are electron-bookkeeping models of one hybrid, not rapidly interconverting molecules.
  • Keep atom connectivity, electron count, and overall charge fixed; check formal charges and valence.
  • Curved arrows track electrons; ↔ links resonance contributors.
  • Equivalent contributors can explain equivalent bonds, as in nitrite and acetate.
  • Use contributor-comparison guidelines together; delocalization is one factor in stability.
