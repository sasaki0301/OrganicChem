Organic Acids and Bases

Use structure, conjugate partners, and approximate pKₐ values to explain why organic molecules differ in their tendency to donate or accept a proton.

Recall Lesson 4: A Brønsted–Lowry acid donates H⁺; a base accepts it. A conjugate pair differs by one proton. Strength, concentration, and pH describe different things.

1. Identify the acid and its conjugate base

An organic Brønsted acid can donate H⁺. Remove the proton from the relevant bond and retain its bonding electron pair on the atom it was attached to. The resulting species is the conjugate base.

Worked example 1 · Proton transfer

Acetic acid and water

CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺

Acetic acid donates the H on its –OH group; water accepts it. The conjugate pairs are CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻ and H₂O/H₃O⁺. The O–H bonding pair stays with oxygen in the acid-to-base drawing, so the conjugate base has overall charge −1.

Acetic acid is weak in water: it partially ionizes at equilibrium. It is nevertheless more acidic than phenol or ethanol under comparable conditions. “Stronger than ethanol” does not mean “a strong acid in water.”

Quick check 1

What is the conjugate base of ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH?

Show answer and explanation
Ethoxide, CH₃CH₂O⁻. Remove H⁺ from O–H; the bonding electron pair remains on oxygen.

2. Use pKₐ to compare acids

For a specified acid dissociation and solvent, pKₐ = −log₁₀Kₐ. A lower pKₐ means a stronger acid under comparable conditions. A one-unit pKₐ difference corresponds to a tenfold difference in Kₐ, not automatically a tenfold difference in the pH of two solutions.

AcidApproximate pKₐConjugate base
Acetic acid, CH₃COOH4.8Acetate, CH₃COO⁻
Phenol, C₆H₅OH10Phenoxide, C₆H₅O⁻
Ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH16Ethoxide, CH₃CH₂O⁻

Introductory approximate aqueous reference values at about 25 °C; values vary with source and conditions. Very weak acids such as ethanol are difficult to measure directly in water, so reported values may be estimated or extrapolated. Do not combine pKₐ values from different solvents as though they were directly interchangeable.

Worked example 2 · Read the numbers

Acetic acid, phenol, or ethanol?

  1. Compare the listed pKₐ values: 4.8, 10, and 16.
  2. Lower pKₐ means stronger acid: acetic acid > phenol > ethanol for these examples.
  3. In the same solvent and comparison, the corresponding base-strength trend is reversed: ethoxide > phenoxide > acetate.

This is a comparison of the named acids; it is not a claim that every carboxylic acid is stronger than every organic acid.

3. Explain a comparison using conjugate bases

For a related series under comparable conditions, increased stabilization of the conjugate base relative to its acid can favor deprotonation. Resonance is one possible contribution; solvation, the original acid, and other structural effects matter too.

Worked example 3 · Acetic acid versus ethanol

Compare acetate and ethoxide

Acetate is represented by two equivalent major contributors:

CH₃–C(=Oa)–Ob⁻   ↔   CH₃–C(–Oa⁻)=Ob

The subscripts a and b track the same oxygens in both drawings. The hybrid delocalizes charge over both oxygens. Ethoxide, CH₃CH₂O⁻, has no comparable carboxylate resonance delocalization of its oxygen charge. This helps explain why acetic acid is more acidic than ethanol.

Do not simply count drawings: contributors can have different importance, and the relevant comparison includes the acid as well as its conjugate base.

Quick check 2 · Phenol

Why is phenol more acidic than a comparable alcohol such as cyclohexanol?

Show answer and explanation
Phenoxide’s negative charge can be delocalized through the aromatic π system in resonance contributors; cyclohexoxide has no directly comparable π-delocalization. Solvation and other effects also contribute.

4. Other structural effects

Worked example 4 · Induction

Chloroacetic acid versus acetic acid

ClCH₂COOH is more acidic than CH₃COOH. Chlorine withdraws electron density through σ bonds by an inductive effect, helping stabilize the conjugate base relative to the acid. The effect generally weakens as the substituent is farther from the charge-bearing group.

Inductive effects can be electron-withdrawing or electron-donating. Compare related structures and consider all relevant effects rather than assuming any electronegative atom always determines the answer.

Worked example 5 · Hybridization

Compare C–H acids with like atoms

For the simple hydrocarbons ethyne HC≡CH, ethene H₂C=CH₂, and ethane H₃C–CH₃, the approximate acidity order is ethyne > ethene > ethane. Deprotonation puts charge on an sp, sp², or sp³ carbon, respectively. Greater s-character helps stabilize charge closer to the nucleus in this comparison.

A terminal alkyne is still a very weak acid in water. This trend applies to these comparable C–H acids; it is not a universal ranking across different elements or unrelated functional groups. An internal alkyne has no terminal ≡C–H proton to remove.

5. Organic bases: where can H⁺ attach?

Many organic bases contain a nitrogen atom with a lone pair that can accept H⁺. The strength of a base depends on its structure, its conjugate acid, the solvent, and competing effects; the mere presence of a lone pair does not guarantee equal basicity.

Worked example 6 · Methylamine and aniline

Compare the nitrogen lone pairs

CH₃NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃NH₃⁺ + OH⁻

Nitrogen in methylamine accepts H⁺ from water. Methylamine is a weak base in water, although it is more basic than aniline under comparable aqueous conditions.

In aniline (C₆H₅NH₂), the nitrogen lone pair can delocalize into the aromatic ring, stabilizing the unprotonated base. Protonation ties up that pair, reducing this delocalization. This helps explain why aniline is less basic than methylamine; solvent and substituent effects also matter.

Some carbanions are very strong bases, but carbanion basicity varies greatly with resonance, nearby electron-withdrawing groups, solvent, and counterions. Avoid labeling every carbanion equally strong.

6. Predict the favored direction

For HA + B⁻ ⇌ A⁻ + HB, identify the acid on each side. When pKₐ values are comparable in the same solvent and under the same conditions, equilibrium generally favors the side with the weaker acid: the one with the higher pKₐ. This predicts equilibrium preference, not reaction speed.

Worked example 7 · Acid and conjugate acid

Acetic acid and hydroxide

CH₃COOH + OH⁻ ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O

  1. The acid on the left is acetic acid (pKₐ ≈ 4.8 in water).
  2. The acid on the right is water (pKₐ ≈ 15.7 in water).
  3. Water is the weaker acid, so the proton-transfer equilibrium strongly favors acetate and water under the introductory aqueous model.
Careful comparison: Solvent, temperature, concentration, and side reactions can affect what is observed. pKₐ values from different media should not be mixed for quantitative equilibrium predictions.

8. Key takeaways

  • Draw the conjugate partner by transferring H⁺ and tracking the bonding electron pair.
  • Lower pKₐ means a stronger acid in a consistent comparison; the corresponding conjugate base is weaker.
  • Resonance, induction, hybridization, solvation, and the original acid all affect the comparison.
  • Carboxylic acids such as acetic acid and amines such as methylamine are weak in water, though their relative strengths differ.
  • In a simple proton-transfer equilibrium, compare the acids on both sides using compatible pKₐ values.
