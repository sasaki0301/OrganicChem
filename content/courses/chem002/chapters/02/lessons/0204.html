Drawing Chemical Structures

A chemical formula can tell you what atoms are present; a structure tells you how they are connected. Learn to translate among representations without losing atoms, bonds, charges, or lone pairs.

Allow about 50–60 minutes, or work in two sessions. Draw the examples on paper before revealing the explanations. Scroll wide tables sideways on small screens.

GoalsRepresentationsLewis structuresSkeletal rulesConversions3D drawingsPracticeSummary

What you will learn

  • Distinguish molecular, Lewis, displayed, condensed, and skeletal formulas.
  • Count valence electrons, lone pairs, and formal charges in simple Lewis structures.
  • Read endpoints, corners, branches, multiple bonds, rings, and heteroatoms in skeletal formulas.
  • Convert representations and check atom counts and valence.
  • Explain what wedge and dash bonds communicate about three-dimensional arrangement.
From earlier lessons: a neutral carbon typically has four bonds, oxygen two, nitrogen three, and hydrogen one in the simple examples below. Charges and exceptions require checking; these are useful starting patterns, not universal rules.

Worked example 1

Follow ethanol across representations

Molecular: C₂H₆O. Condensed: CH₃CH₂OH. The first C has 3 H, the second has 2 H, and the O–H group contributes the sixth H.

Fully displayed Lewis drawing: all atoms and bonds are shown; green dots show oxygen’s two lone pairs.

Skeletal drawing: numbered labels 1 and 2 are teaching aids, not atom symbols or part of the formula. The labeled OH is an oxygen with an attached hydrogen; it is not a third carbon.

A molecular formula does not uniquely determine connectivity. Ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH, and dimethyl ether, CH₃OCH₃, both have C₂H₆O but different connections. They are constitutional isomers; the formula alone does not decide which one was intended.

2. Draw Lewis structures with an electron budget

Use the method from Lesson 2: (1) total valence electrons and adjust for charge; (2) connect atoms with single bonds; (3) place remaining electrons to fill appropriate shells; (4) consider multiple bonds if needed; (5) check total electrons and formal charges. Each line represents one shared pair.

Worked example 2

Water: bonds and lone pairs

  1. Count: O contributes 6 and two H contribute 2, for 8 valence electrons.
  2. Connect H–O–H: two bonds use 4 electrons.
  3. Put the remaining 4 on O as two lone pairs.
  4. Check: 4 bonding + 4 nonbonding = 8; O has an octet and each H a duet.

O has two bonding pairs and two lone pairs. The molecule’s shape is bent; a flat Lewis arrangement alone does not encode the exact angle.

Worked example 3

BF₄⁻: a charged Lewis structure

  1. Count electrons: B contributes 3; four F contribute 4(7) = 28; add 1 for the −1 charge. Total = 32.
  2. Place B in the center with four B–F single bonds: 8 electrons used.
  3. Place the remaining 24 electrons as three lone pairs on each F.
  4. Enclose the ion in brackets and show the overall − charge. B has four bonds and no lone pair in this contributor.

Formal charges: B = 3 − 0 − 4 = −1; each F = 7 − 6 − 1 = 0. The sum matches the ion charge, −1. The 2D cross shows connectivity; the ion’s molecular shape is tetrahedral.

This diagram is BF₄⁻: the central symbol is B, not Br.

Worked example 4

Nitrite, NO₂⁻: two resonance contributors

  1. Total valence electrons: 5 + 2(6) + 1 = 18.
  2. Use O–N–O. Complete octets, converting one O lone pair into a second N–O bond.
  3. N has one lone pair. In each contributor, the single-bonded O has three lone pairs and formal charge −1; the double-bonded O has two lone pairs and formal charge 0. N has formal charge 0.
  4. Switch the placement of the double bond to obtain the second contributor. The atom connections stay the same.

3. Read line-angle formulas correctly

  1. Unlabeled line ends and corners: each represents a carbon atom. An unlabeled branch junction is also a carbon.
  2. Carbon hydrogens: add enough H to give each ordinary neutral carbon a total bond order of four. A double bond counts as 2; a triple as 3.
  3. Heteroatoms: symbols such as O, N, Cl, and their charges are written. Hydrogens attached to heteroatoms are normally written too, for example OH or NH₂.
  4. Multiple lines and rings: preserve double/triple bonds; each unlabeled corner of a closed polygon is a carbon.
  5. Exceptions: explicit carbon labels may be used for emphasis. Charges, radicals, and unusual valences need explicit treatment rather than blindly adding H.
A labeled O or Cl at a line end replaces the implied carbon at that end. An unlabeled terminus at the end of a multiple bond is still a carbon. An empty bend created only by a drawing label is not an additional atom.
Worked example 5

Read a four-carbon zigzag

  1. Count endpoints and corners: 1, 2, 3, and 4 → four carbons.
  2. All connections are single bonds. End carbons have one C–C bond → CH₃ each.
  3. Each interior carbon has two C–C bonds → CH₂ each.
  4. Write CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃; add H: 3 + 2 + 2 + 3 = 10. Molecular formula: C₄H₁₀.

Green numbers are teaching labels, not atom symbols or chain-naming instructions.

Worked example 6

Read a branch junction

  1. Count the three endpoints and the central junction → 4 carbon atoms.
  2. The central carbon has three single bonds to other carbons, so it has one H: CH.
  3. Each endpoint is CH₃. A condensed formula is CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃, also written (CH₃)₃CH.
  4. H total: 3 + 1 + 3 + 3 = 10 → C₄H₁₀.

Numbers are only identification aids. This branched compound and straight-chain butane have the same molecular formula but different connections.

Worked example 7

Read a double bond

  1. There are three carbon positions. The parallel lines form one double bond between carbon positions 1 and 2; they do not introduce extra carbon atoms.
  2. Left end: bond order 2 → CH₂. Middle: bond order 2 + 1 = 3 → CH. Right end: bond order 1 → CH₃.
  3. Condensed: CH₂=CHCH₃. Molecular formula: C₃H₆.

A skeletal drawing is a plain six-sided ring with single bonds and no substituents. How many carbons and hydrogens does it represent?

4. Convert in both directions

Worked example 8

From condensed formula to a skeletal drawing

CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃

  1. Identify four carbon groups and their sequence: C–C–C–C.
  2. Draw three connected single-bond segments. Each new segment adds one carbon position to the chain.
  3. Check the endpoints have one bond each and the interior positions two; implied hydrogens give CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃.

Rotating or reflecting the zigzag on the page does not create a new compound. Connectivity, bond order, charge, and any specified stereochemistry matter.

Branches in parentheses: in CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃, the CH₃ in parentheses is attached to the immediately preceding CH. Parentheses show connectivity, not a separate disconnected molecule.

A line-angle drawing has two connected carbon positions and then a line to the written group OH. How many carbons are present? Give a condensed formula.

5. Add three-dimensional information

Two-dimensional formulas are useful maps of connectivity, but ordinary lines alone do not specify the full spatial arrangement. In wedge-and-dash notation, a plain line is drawn in the page plane, a solid wedge projects toward the viewer, and a hashed wedge projects away.

Schematic tetrahedral center. This drawing specifies the arrangement shown; it does not by itself supply an R/S name. Different orientations on a screen can describe the same 3D structure.

Stereochemical drawings can distinguish molecules with the same connectivity but different spatial arrangements. A conventional skeletal formula without wedges does not automatically specify which stereoisomer is present.

Guided drill 7 · Revisit ethanol bonding

In the simple localized model, what hybridization is assigned to each carbon and the oxygen in ethanol? Why is O–H polar?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Each carbon has four single-bond regions, so each is described as sp³. Oxygen has two bonds and two lone pairs, giving four regions and an sp³ approximation; its local atom arrangement is bent. Oxygen attracts shared electron density more strongly than hydrogen, making O–H polar with O δ− and H δ+.

B. Explain and diagnose · Items 7–12

7 · Molecular formula versus structure

A student says ethanol and dimethyl ether must be the same substance because both are C₂H₆O. Explain the error.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

They have the same atom counts but different connectivity: CH₃CH₂OH versus CH₃OCH₃. They are constitutional isomers.

8 · A labeled endpoint

A student counts three carbons in the skeletal ethanol drawing because there are three positions along the chain. What is the error?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

The third labeled position is O, not C. The drawing has two carbon positions followed by OH.

9 · Multiple bonds

In CH₂=CH₂, how many electrons are represented by the double bond? How many σ and π bonds does that connection contain?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

A double bond represents two shared pairs, or 4 electrons, and consists of 1 σ + 1 π in the localized model. The double connection is one electron region around each carbon.

10 · BF₄⁻ bookkeeping

A student counts 31 valence electrons for BF₄⁻ and leaves the ion unbracketed. Identify both corrections.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Add one electron for the negative charge: 3 + 4(7) + 1 = 32. Enclose the complete ion structure in brackets and write the overall − charge outside.

11 · Nitrite resonance

Are the two nitrite resonance contributors different compounds that repeatedly switch places?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

No. They are two ways to describe electron distribution in the same ion; the actual structure is a resonance hybrid. The atomic connectivity is unchanged.

12 · Wedge and dash

What three-dimensional information is lost if the wedge and hashed wedge in the earlier example are replaced by plain lines?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

The connectivity remains, but the explicit indication of which bond points toward or away from the viewer is lost. Depending on the molecule, that may leave stereochemistry unspecified.

7. What to remember

  • Lewis: count total valence electrons, then check bonds, lone pairs, and charge.
  • Condensed: parentheses identify branches attached to the preceding group.
  • Skeletal: count unlabeled ends, vertices, and junctions as carbons; write heteroatoms explicitly.
  • Carbon H: use bond order to complete ordinary neutral carbon’s valence of four.
  • 3D: wedges and hashes convey spatial information; plain formulas may not specify stereochemistry.

Key terms

TermMeaning
Lewis structureA valence-electron representation showing bonding and lone pairs, with charges where needed.
Condensed formulaA compact way to write connected atom groups.
Skeletal formulaA structure where most carbon labels and C–H bonds are implied.
HeteroatomAn atom other than carbon or hydrogen in an organic structure.
Constitutional isomersCompounds with the same molecular formula and different connectivity.
Resonance contributorOne electron-placement drawing used to represent a delocalized structure with the same atomic connectivity.

These conventions prepare you to identify parent chains and substituents in later naming lessons.
