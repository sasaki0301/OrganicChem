Review of Atomic Structure

Why can carbon form the framework of so many molecules? Start with the atom: its protons identify the element, its neutrons distinguish isotopes, and its electrons help explain bonding.

1. Identify the particles and their roles

An atom has a small, dense nucleus containing protons and usually neutrons. Electrons occupy states described by orbitals in the space around the nucleus. Nearly all atomic mass is in the nucleus.

Three particles to track
ParticleRelative chargeLocationApproximate mass
Proton, p+1Nucleus1 u
Neutron, n0Nucleus1 u
Electron, e−1Orbitals around the nucleusAbout 1/1836 of a proton’s mass

Here, u means atomic mass unit. Relative charges are measured in units of the elementary charge. Ordinary hydrogen-1 has no neutron.

Model check: Bohr-style circles can help organize shell counts, but electrons in multi-electron atoms do not follow the fixed circular tracks shown in that model. An orbital is a quantum description of an electron’s spatial state; its probability distribution tells us where the electron may be found.

Quick check · Which particle matters?

Which particle count identifies the element? Which particles are gained or lost when a simple ion forms?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Protons identify the element. Gaining or losing electrons forms a simple ion. Ordinary ion formation does not change the nucleus.

2. Count particles in a neutral atom or ion

Read the symbol before calculating. Z is the atomic number, A is the mass number, and the charge appears at the upper right.

2311Na+
Sodium-23 ion
A = 23 · Z = 11 · charge = +1
p = Zn = A − Zcharge = p − e

In the charge equation, use the signed charge number: +1 for Na⁺, −1 for Cl⁻, and −2 for O²⁻. Therefore, e = Z − charge. A neutral atom has charge 0, so only then does e = Z.

Worked example 1

Compare neutral sodium-23 with Na⁺

  1. Find protons: Z = 11, so p = 11 in both species.
  2. Find neutrons: n = 23 − 11 = 12 in both.
  3. Use the charge: neutral Na has e = 11 − 0 = 11; Na⁺ has e = 11 − (+1) = 10.
  4. Check: for Na⁺, p − e = 11 − 10 = +1. One electron was lost; the element is still sodium.
Worked example 2

Count particles in oxygen-16, O²⁻

Given A = 16, Z = 8, and charge = −2:

  1. p = 8.
  2. n = 16 − 8 = 8.
  3. e = 8 − (−2) = 10.
  4. Check: 8 − 10 = −2. The ion has two more electrons than protons.
Remember the direction: a positive ion (cation) has lost electrons; a negative ion (anion) has gained electrons. Do not add protons to explain a positive charge in ordinary ion formation.

3. Separate isotopes, ions, and different elements

Isotopes have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Ions have unequal proton and electron counts. These are separate ideas: a particular isotope can be neutral or charged.

Worked example 3

What changes—and what stays the same?

Compare 3517Cl and 3717Cl−.

  1. Same element: both have 17 protons.
  2. Different isotopes: neutron counts are 35 − 17 = 18 and 37 − 17 = 20.
  3. Different charge states: neutral chlorine-35 has 17 electrons; chloride-37 has 18.

These two species differ in both neutron count and electron count. Neither difference changes their identity as chlorine.

Mass number is not average atomic mass. A is a whole-number count of protons plus neutrons for one isotope. A periodic-table value such as chlorine’s approximate 35.45 is an isotope-weighted average atomic mass, not the mass number of one atom. Do not round it to invent a neutron count; use the isotope’s stated A.

Guided drill 2 · Classify the difference

Species X has 6 p, 7 n, and 6 e. Species Y has 6 p, 8 n, and 6 e. Species Z has 7 p, 7 n, and 7 e. Which two are isotopes of the same element?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

X and Y are carbon-13 and carbon-14: same six protons, different neutrons. Z is nitrogen-14 because it has seven protons. Y and Z have the same mass number but are different elements.

4. Organize electrons: shell → subshell → orbital

An electron configuration records how electrons occupy subshells. A ground state is the lowest-energy arrangement for that atom or ion.

Shell

A principal level labelled n = 1, 2, 3, … . A shell contains subshells. The number in 2p identifies the shell.

Subshell

A set of orbitals of a particular type, such as s or p. The letter in 2p identifies the subshell type.

Orbital: an individual quantum state with a characteristic spatial distribution. Each orbital can hold at most two electrons. Do not confuse an orbital with an orbit or track.

Subshells needed for this lesson
Subshell typeOrbitals per subshellMaximum electrons
s12
p36

The first shell has only 1s. The second shell has 2s and 2p, so it can hold 2 + 6 = 8 electrons. Higher shells contain additional subshell types; “every shell holds eight” is not a general rule.

2p4
2 = shell · p = subshell · 4 = electrons in that subshell

Three rules for building the ground state

  1. Fill lower-energy orbitals first (Aufbau principle). For neutral atoms through argon, use 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p.
  2. At most two electrons per orbital, with opposite spins (Pauli exclusion principle). In the diagrams, one box is one orbital; each arrow represents one electron.
  3. Occupy equal-energy orbitals singly before pairing (Hund’s rule). For the three p orbitals of an isolated atom, place one parallel-spin electron in each before adding opposite-spin partners.

Arrows indicate spin states; they do not show electrons moving upward or downward. This filling sequence is limited to the examples here, not a complete rule for every element.

5. Write, check, and interpret a configuration

Use this routine: count electrons → fill subshells in order → add the superscripts → identify the highest occupied shell. For an ion, adjust the electron count first.
Worked example 4

Carbon: from atomic number to valence electrons

  1. Count: neutral carbon has Z = 6, so 6 electrons.
  2. Allocate: 1s takes 2; 2s takes 2; the remaining 2 go into 2p.
  3. Write: 1s2 2s2 2p2.
  4. Check total: 2 + 2 + 2 = 6.
  5. Find valence electrons: the highest occupied shell is n = 2. Count both 2s and 2p: 2 + 2 = 4 valence electrons.

Worked example 6

Chlorine and chloride: the ion has one extra electron

Neutral chlorine has 17 electrons:

Cl: 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p5

Check total: 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 5 = 17. The outer n = 3 shell contains 2 + 5 = 7 valence electrons.

Cl⁻ has 17 − (−1) = 18 electrons:

Cl⁻: 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6

Its outer shell is filled in this configuration. The proton count stays 17, so it remains chlorine.

Why this matters in organic chemistry

For the main-group atoms below, valence electrons are those in the highest occupied principal shell. They are central to later Lewis structures and bonding models. Count the entire outer shell, not only the last subshell written.

Guided drill 4 · Complete the routine

Neutral nitrogen has Z = 7. Write its configuration, check the sum, and identify the number of valence electrons.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Configuration: 1s² 2s² 2p³. Total: 2 + 2 + 3 = 7. The highest occupied shell is n = 2, so valence electrons = 2 + 3 = 5. Its three 2p electrons occupy separate orbitals in the ground state.

Guided drill 5 · An ion can share a configuration with another element

Na has Z = 11. Write the configuration of Na⁺. It matches neutral neon (Z = 10); does that make the ion neon?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Na⁺ has 10 electrons: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. It has the same electron configuration as neutral Ne but still has 11 protons, so it is sodium. Such species are called isoelectronic: they have the same number of electrons.

C. Explain and diagnose · Items 9–12

9 · Same mass number, same element?

Species A has 6 p, 8 n, 6 e. Species B has 7 p, 7 n, 7 e. Are they isotopes of the same element?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

No. Both have mass number 14, but A is carbon-14 and B is nitrogen-14. Isotopes must have the same proton count.

10 · Find the impossible subshell count

A student writes oxygen as 1s² 2s⁴ 2p². The superscripts add to eight. Is it valid? Correct it.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

No. An s subshell has one orbital and holds at most two electrons. The correct ground-state configuration is 1s² 2s² 2p⁴. A correct total alone is not enough.

11 · Correct the valence count

A student says carbon has two valence electrons because its configuration ends in 2p². Explain the error.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

The outer shell is all of n = 2, including 2s² and 2p². Carbon therefore has four valence electrons. The last subshell is not the entire outer shell.

12 · Test a particle claim

An ion has 8 protons, 10 neutrons, and 10 electrons. Identify its element, mass number, and charge. Would removing one electron turn it into nitrogen?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

It is oxygen-18, O²⁻: Z = 8, A = 8 + 10 = 18, and charge = 8 − 10 = −2. Removing one electron would leave 9 electrons and charge −1. It would still be oxygen because its eight protons remain.

7. Bring the ideas together

  • Identity: protons determine the element.
  • Isotope: neutron count changes A while Z stays the same.
  • Charge: compare protons and electrons; charge = p − e.
  • Electron arrangement: obey subshell capacities, filling order, Pauli, and Hund.
  • Valence: for the main-group examples here, count electrons in the highest occupied shell.
