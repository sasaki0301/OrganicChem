Hybridization, Sigma and Pi Bonds

Why can a carbon chain bend at some bonds but resist twisting at others? Use electron regions, a local orbital model, and a complete bond count to connect structure to behavior.

What you will learn

  • Count electron regions around a specified atom and assign a simple local sp, sp², or sp³ model.
  • Distinguish electron-region geometry from the shape formed by atoms.
  • Describe σ and π bonding and count all bonds in a molecule, including C–H bonds.
  • Connect π overlap with restricted rotation while recognizing the limits of the model.
Recall Lesson 2: each bond—single, double, or triple—counts as one electron region around a particular atom. Each lone pair adds one region. This is different from counting individual σ and π bonds.

1. What does hybridization describe?

In a valence-bond model, hybridization combines atomic orbitals on the same atom into hybrid orbitals oriented for bonding. It is a useful model for describing localized bonds and approximate geometry; it is not a claim that we can directly watch orbitals physically mix.

Orbital number is conserved: combining one s orbital with three p orbitals produces four sp³ hybrid orbitals. For a simple carbon description, sp² leaves one p orbital unhybridized; sp leaves two. Those unhybridized p orbitals can participate in π bonding.

Angles are ideal reference values. Actual angles depend on the atoms, lone pairs, and molecular environment. The sp / sp² / sp³ assignment by region count is a useful introductory approximation; resonance and delocalization can require a more careful orbital model.
Worked example 1

Why use sp³ for carbon in methane?

  1. CH₄ has four C–H bonds and no lone pair on C: four electron regions.
  2. VSEPR predicts tetrahedral arrangement; the H–C–H angle is about 109.5°.
  3. In the localized hybrid model, carbon uses four equivalent sp³ orbitals, each overlapping with a hydrogen 1s orbital to describe one σ bond.
  4. Four C–H bonds means 4 σ and 0 π bonds.

Some introductory models represent electron promotion as an accounting step before hybridization. Treat it as a model for bonding, not as evidence that an isolated carbon atom must undergo a separately observed step before every reaction.

Guided drill 1 · Conserve orbitals

If one s orbital combines with two p orbitals on carbon, how many hybrid orbitals result, and how many valence p orbitals remain unhybridized?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Three orbitals combine to give three sp² orbitals. One of the three original valence p orbitals remains unhybridized. The number of orbitals is conserved.

2. Assign hybridization to one atom at a time

Routine: draw the Lewis structure → choose the atom → count atoms directly attached to it plus its lone pairs → map 2, 3, or 4 regions to sp, sp², or sp³ in this introductory model → check electron geometry and local bonding.

Count one region per bonded neighbor, even when the bond is double or triple. Add one region for each lone pair on the chosen atom. Do not count atoms elsewhere in the molecule or lone pairs on neighboring atoms.

In BF₃, boron has three regions and a trigonal-planar sp² model; it has six electrons around it, an example of an incomplete octet. For NH₃, four regions have an approximately tetrahedral electron-region arrangement; the molecular shape, which names atom positions, is trigonal pyramidal. H₂O likewise has four regions but a bent molecular shape. Lone pairs affect bond angles.

Worked example 2

Two different carbons in propene

CH₂=CH–CH₃

  1. The left CH₂ carbon has two H neighbors and one C neighbor: 3 regions → sp².
  2. The middle CH carbon has one H and two C neighbors: 3 regions → sp².
  3. The right CH₃ carbon has three H and one C neighbor: 4 regions → sp³.

Assign each carbon separately. The double bond counts as one region at each carbon it joins.

Guided drill 2 · Double bonds and regions

In CO₂, the central carbon is double-bonded to two oxygens. How many electron regions surround carbon? Give its local hybridization and electron-region geometry.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Each double bond is one region, so carbon has 2 regions. The introductory model is sp and the electron-region geometry is linear (180°).

Guided drill 3 · Lone pairs change shape

NH₃ has three N–H bonds and one lone pair on nitrogen. Assign the simple hybridization and distinguish its electron-region geometry from its molecular shape.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Three bonds + one lone pair = 4 regions, giving an sp³ approximation and a tetrahedral electron-region arrangement. The molecular shape is trigonal pyramidal, not tetrahedral.

3. Sigma and pi bonds: two overlap patterns

A σ bond has electron density concentrated along the internuclear axis and can be described by end-on overlap. A π bond has a nodal plane containing that axis and can be described, in these examples, by sideways overlap of parallel unhybridized p orbitals.

For ordinary localized single, double, and triple bonds
Connectionσ bondsπ bondsShared pairs
Single: A–B101
Double: A=B112
Triple: A≡B123
For a whole-molecule count: count one σ for every connected pair of bonded atoms. Then add one π for each double bond and two π for each triple bond. Include bonds to hydrogen. This shortcut applies to the ordinary localized structures used here.

In the localized model of a C=C bond, each sp² carbon has one unhybridized p orbital. Their sideways overlap makes one π bond; its electron density has regions above and below the σ framework, which are parts of the same π bond, not two π bonds.

For C≡C, each sp carbon has two unhybridized p orbitals. Two perpendicular pairs overlap to form two π bonds, in addition to the C–C σ bond.

Guided drill 4 · Count the connection

How many σ and π bonds make up a single C≡C connection? How many electron regions does that connection count as around either carbon?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

The triple connection contains 1 σ + 2 π, but it is one electron region around either carbon. The two counting tasks answer different questions.

4. Worked examples: include every hydrogen

The diagrams display all C–H bonds. A line represents a shared electron pair; multiple lines connect the same pair of atoms. Each diagram is a schematic structure, not a complete three-dimensional orbital picture.

Worked example 3 · Ethane

CH₃–CH₃: single bond

  1. Each carbon has four neighbors → 4 regions → sp³.
  2. Count six C–H connections → 6 σ.
  3. Count one C–C single connection → 1 σ, 0 π.
  4. Whole molecule: 7 σ, 0 π.
Worked example 4 · Ethene

CH₂=CH₂: double bond

  1. Each carbon has two H neighbors and one C neighbor → 3 regions → sp².
  2. The four C–H bonds contribute 4 σ.
  3. The C=C connection contributes 1 σ + 1 π.
  4. Whole molecule: 5 σ, 1 π.

The C=C connection alone is 1 σ + 1 π. The four C–H σ bonds must be added to obtain the whole-molecule total.

Worked example 5 · Ethyne

HC≡CH: triple bond

  1. Each carbon has one H neighbor and one C neighbor → 2 regions → sp.
  2. Two C–H bonds contribute 2 σ.
  3. The C≡C connection contributes 1 σ + 2 π.
  4. Whole molecule: 3 σ, 2 π.
Worked example 6 · Propene

CH₂=CH–CH₃: different carbon environments

The six C–H bonds contribute 6 σ. The two carbon–carbon connections contribute 2 σ; the double bond adds 1 π. Total: 8 σ and 1 π. The two alkene carbons are sp²; the CH₃ carbon is sp³.

Guided drill 5 · Carbonyl bonds count too

Methanal is H₂C=O. Determine the hybridization of carbon and count σ and π bonds in the whole molecule.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Carbon has two H neighbors and one O neighbor: 3 regions → sp². Two C–H bonds give 2 σ; C=O gives 1 σ + 1 π. Total = 3 σ and 1 π.

5. Why double bonds resist twisting

In the localized π model of ethene, effective overlap needs parallel p orbitals. Twisting around the C=C axis reduces their alignment and raises the energy; a 90° twist would disrupt the π overlap. This helps explain the rotational barrier and can allow distinct geometric isomers when each double-bonded carbon has two different substituents.

A single C–C σ bond can often rotate more readily without losing its end-on overlap, although other interactions can create rotational barriers. Some single bonds—including bonds with partial double-bond character—also have restricted rotation. A double bond does not automatically produce cis/trans isomers: the substituents must satisfy the stated condition.

Guided drill 6 · Avoid a common shortcut

Ethene itself has H₂C=CH₂. Can it have cis/trans isomers simply because it has a C=C bond?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

No. Each carbon has two identical hydrogen substituents. Each double-bonded carbon must have two different substituents for the usual geometric-isomer distinction.

9 · Catch the ethene error

A learner reports 1 σ and 1 π for all of C₂H₄. Which bonds were omitted?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

The learner counted only C=C. Four C–H σ bonds were omitted. The correct molecular total is 5 σ and 1 π.

10 · Count orbitals, not electrons

A student claims that one s and two p orbitals make two sp² orbitals. Correct the count and identify the remaining p orbital.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Three orbitals mix to give three sp² orbitals; one of the original three valence p orbitals remains unhybridized.

11 · Geometry and lone pairs

Why can oxygen in H₂O be described by an sp³ model even though the molecule is bent rather than tetrahedral in molecular shape?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

O has two bonding regions and two lone pairs: four regions give a roughly tetrahedral electron-region arrangement. The molecular shape considers only atom positions, so it is bent. The sp³ description is an introductory approximation.

12 · Predict the effect of twisting

Why does a large twist about ethene’s C=C bond cost energy in the localized orbital picture?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Twisting misaligns the parallel p orbitals and reduces the sideways overlap that supports the π bond. The resulting loss of π bonding raises the energy.

7. Bring the ideas together

Lewis structure→ chosen atom→ electron regions→ local model→ whole-molecule σ / π count
  • 2, 3, 4 regions: commonly sp, sp², sp³ respectively in the simple localized model.
  • One connected atom pair: one σ; a double adds one π and a triple adds two.
  • Whole-molecule totals: include every H bond and every multiple bond.
  • Shape: electron-region arrangement includes lone pairs; molecular shape describes atom positions.
  • Model limits: actual angles vary, and resonance can complicate a simple local assignment.

Key terms

TermMeaning here
Hybrid orbitalAn orbital constructed by combining atomic orbitals on the same atom in a bonding model.
Electron regionA bonding connection or lone pair around the chosen atom; a multiple bond is one region.
σ bondA bond described by end-on overlap and density along the internuclear axis.
π bondA bond with a nodal plane containing the internuclear axis; in these examples, described by sideways p overlap.
Unhybridized p orbitalA p orbital not included in the selected hybrid set; it can participate in π bonding.
