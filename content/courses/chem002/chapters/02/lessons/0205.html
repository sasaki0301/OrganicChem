Organic Nomenclature: An IUPAC Workflow

Turn a structure into a clear name by choosing a parent, numbering it, identifying substituents, and assembling the parts. Then reverse the process to check that the name describes the original structure.

Allow about 50–60 minutes, or work in two sessions. Draw and number each structure before opening the answer. Scroll diagrams and wide tables sideways on small screens.

GoalsName partsWorkflowWorked examplesFunctional groupsName → structurePracticeSummary

What you will learn

  • Identify the parent, prefix, locants, and ending in a simple systematic name.
  • Name selected open-chain alkanes, haloalkanes, alkenes, and alcohols.
  • Compare numbering directions at the first point of difference and alphabetize simple prefixes.
  • Connect a name to a drawing, then verify carbon counts, branch positions, and bonding.
Scope: this is an introductory workflow for the simple open-chain examples shown. Rings, multiple characteristic groups, complex substituents, stereochemical descriptors, and competing parent-selection cases require additional rules. More detailed alkane naming follows in the next chapter.

1. Take apart a name

A systematic name can describe substituents, the parent skeleton, bond unsaturation, and a principal characteristic group. Not every component occurs in every name.

3-chloro-2-methylpentane
  • Prefixes: chloro and methyl identify groups attached to the parent. Their numbers are locants.
  • Parent root: pent indicates five carbons in the selected parent chain.
  • Ending: -ane identifies a saturated parent in this simple example. An alkene has an -ene ending; an alcohol may use -ol as the principal characteristic-group suffix.
Keep the roles separate: -ane and -ene describe saturation or unsaturation of the parent; -ol, -al, -one, -oic acid, and -amine can identify a principal characteristic group. An alkene can also have a principal-group suffix, as in prop-2-en-1-ol.

Guided drill 1 · Read the name

In 3-chloro-2-methylpentane, how many carbons are in the parent? Which groups are attached, and at which positions?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

The parent is pentane: 5 carbons. A methyl group is attached at C-2 and a chloro group at C-3. The methyl adds one carbon, so the entire molecule has six carbons.

2. Use a repeatable naming workflow

  1. Read the structure: expand condensed branches and mark the principal characteristic group, if any, and any multiple bonds.
  2. Select an eligible parent: for the simple cases here, select a continuous chain containing the principal group and the shown multiple bond where applicable. Among eligible chains in these examples, use the longest. Do not choose a longer chain that omits the group used as suffix.
  3. Compare numbering directions: in these simple examples, first give the suffix group its lowest locant; if tied or absent, give a multiple bond its lowest locant; then compare substituent locants at the first difference. A double-bond locant identifies the lower-numbered carbon of that bond.
  4. Name and locate substituents: use alkyl or halo prefixes and list one locant for each occurrence. Use di-, tri-, or tetra- for repeated identical simple groups.
  5. Assemble: alphabetize different simple substituent names, ignoring di-, tri-, and tetra- for alphabetization. Add the parent and the appropriate ending.
  6. Check by redrawing: confirm the parent length, every branch position, bond order, valence, and any stereochemistry specified.
Important scope: the complete IUPAC parent-selection and numbering hierarchy includes further criteria and exceptions. In more complex compounds, a blanket instruction to choose “the longest chain containing every double bond” can fail. Apply the full rules when competing chain lengths, unsaturations, rings, or several principal groups arise.

Numbering: compare in order, not by the sum

Arrange the competing substituent locants in increasing order and compare the first position where they differ. For 2,2,4 versus 2,4,4, the first entries tie; the second entries are 2 versus 4, so 2,2,4 wins.

Punctuation: commas separate numbers; hyphens separate numbers and words. Write 2,2-dimethylpropane, not 2-2-dimethyl propane.

Guided drill 2 · First difference

For an alkane parent with no higher-priority feature, compare locant sets 2,3 and 3,4. Which numbering is preferred?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

2,3: compare the first entries, 2 versus 3. The lower number at the first difference decides this comparison.

4. Choose a principal characteristic group

In a multifunctional molecule, a senior characteristic group can be expressed as a suffix; other groups may be named as prefixes. The following is a limited teaching subset, not the complete priority order:

GroupCommon structural featureSuffix in a simple namePrefix when subordinate
Carboxylic acid–COOH-oic aciddepends on context
Aldehyde–CHO-aloften oxo- in suitable structures
Ketone>C=O-oneoxo-
Alcohol–OH-olhydroxy-
Amine–NH₂-amineamino-
Halo substituent–F, –Cl, –Br, –Iusually cited as a prefixfluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo-

Within this limited set, a carboxylic acid is senior to an aldehyde, ketone, alcohol, or amine. Do not treat this short list as a substitute for the complete IUPAC hierarchy. The –COOH carbon is part of the parent and is C-1 in the simple open-chain acid examples here.

Worked example

F. Number the –OH group

Blue = chosen parent; orange = group attached to it; green numbers = parent locants. Scroll the diagram sideways on a small screen.

  1. The three-carbon chain contains –OH: root prop-.
  2. The –OH is on carbon 2 from either end. The principal-group locant is 2.
  3. Use the alcohol suffix and omit the final “e” of propane before -ol: propan-2-ol. The older placement 2-propanol is also common.
Worked example

G. Acid and alcohol together

HOOC–CH(OH)–CH₃

  1. The acid is senior to the alcohol in this pair; the parent has three carbons including the carboxyl carbon.
  2. The carboxyl carbon is C-1; the –OH is on C-2.
  3. Use hydroxy- as the prefix and -oic acid as the suffix: 2-hydroxypropanoic acid.

This name does not specify a stereoisomer; a complete description of a particular stereoisomer requires the appropriate descriptor.

Guided drill 4 · Group priority

A molecule contains both –COOH and –OH. Which group supplies the suffix in this teaching subset, and what prefix can describe the –OH?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

The carboxylic acid supplies -oic acid; the subordinate –OH can be cited as hydroxy-.

5. Work backward from a name

Worked example

Draw 3-chloro-2-methylpentane

  1. Parent: draw five carbons in a continuous single-bond chain: pentane.
  2. Number: label the chain 1 through 5.
  3. Place groups: attach CH₃ at C-2 and Cl at C-3.
  4. Complete valences: add hydrogens so each ordinary neutral C has four bonds.
  5. Check: the condensed structure is CH₃CH(CH₃)CH(Cl)CH₂CH₃.

Recount: five parent carbons plus one methyl carbon = six total carbons. The compound has a stereogenic center; the name shown leaves its configuration unspecified.

Guided drill 5 · Reverse a name

Draw 2,2-dimethylpropane. How many parent carbons, methyl groups, and total carbons are present?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

The parent has 3 carbons. Attach two methyl groups to C-2. Total carbons = 3 + 2 = 5. A condensed structure is CH₃C(CH₃)₂CH₃. The central carbon has four C–C bonds and no H.

12 · A double-bond locant

Why is CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ named but-1-ene rather than but-3-ene?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Number from the end nearer the double bond. It lies between C-1 and C-2, and the smaller of those two numbers is its locant: 1.

7. Quick reference

  • Parent: choose a continuous eligible chain; check the principal group and relevant parent-selection criteria first.
  • Numbering: in these examples, suffix group → multiple bond → substituent locants; compare at the first difference.
  • Prefixes: identify and locate each substituent; use di-, tri-, tetra- for repetitions.
  • Alphabetization: ignore simple multiplicative prefixes when ordering substituent names.
  • Check: reconstruct the structure. Names may need stereochemical descriptors to identify a particular stereoisomer.

Key terms

TermMeaning
Parent chainThe selected continuous chain forming the basis of the name in these examples.
