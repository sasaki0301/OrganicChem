Chemical Bonding Theory

Use valence electrons to explain how atoms connect, then use structure to explain shape, polarity, and properties. The aim is to justify a prediction—not only attach a bond-type label.

Allow about 45–60 minutes, or study in two parts. Draw the structures on paper before revealing answers. On small screens, scroll wide tables sideways.

What you will learn

  • Distinguish ionic, covalent, and metallic bonding using particles and attractions.

  • Use electron regions and electronegativity to explain shape and molecular polarity.
  • Explain coordinate bond formation and interpret bond-length and bond-energy comparisons.
Recall Lesson 1: neutral H contributes 1 valence electron; C contributes 4; N contributes 5; O contributes 6; Cl contributes 7. For a negative ion add electrons to the total; for a positive ion subtract them.

1. Bond formation is an energy question

A stable bond corresponds to a lower-energy arrangement than the relevant separated particles. Attractions between positive nuclei and electrons compete with electron–electron and nucleus–nucleus repulsions. At the equilibrium bond length, the balance gives a minimum in potential energy.

Form a bond

Forming a bond from the corresponding separated particles releases energy.

Break a bond

Separating bonded particles requires energy. A reaction’s net energy change depends on both bonds broken and bonds formed.

Use the octet rule as a guide

Many common structures give second-period atoms such as C, N, and O eight electrons around each atom, counting shared pairs toward both bonded atoms. Hydrogen follows a duet: two electrons, usually in one bond.

The octet is not a universal law or an energy calculation. Electron-deficient species, radicals, and some heavier-element structures need additional treatment. In this lesson, focus on ordinary closed-shell structures of H, C, N, O, and Cl.

Removing an electron from an isolated sodium atom costs energy—even though Na⁺ has a filled outer shell. Formation of solid NaCl is explained by the energy balance of the entire process, including strong attractions in the ionic lattice.

Guided drill 1 · Correct the energy claim

A student says, “Breaking a bond releases energy because the atoms become free.” Correct the statement.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Breaking a bond requires energy to separate attracted particles. Bond formation releases energy. An overall reaction releases energy only when its energy-releasing changes outweigh the energy-requiring changes.

2. Ionic bonding: ions attract in a lattice

An ionic bond is electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. Electron transfer is a useful way to account for formation of simple ions; it is not itself the definition of the attraction that holds an ionic solid together.

Worked example 1

Account for NaCl formation

  1. Track electrons: Na loses one electron, giving Na⁺; Cl gains one, giving Cl⁻.
  2. Identify attraction: positive and negative ions attract electrostatically.
  3. Describe the solid: NaCl forms an extended three-dimensional lattice, not a collection of isolated Na–Cl molecules.
  4. Check charge balance: (+1) + (−1) = 0, so the simplest ratio is 1:1.
Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

These are electron-accounting steps for atoms, not the full reaction or a claim that both steps release energy. The overall elemental reaction is 2Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2NaCl(s).

Use charge balance to find a formula

Mg²⁺ needs two Cl⁻ ions: (+2) + 2(−1) = 0. The formula is MgCl₂, not MgCl. A formula unit gives the simplest whole-number ion ratio.

Connect the model to a property

Solid NaCl has ions fixed in its lattice, so it conducts poorly. When molten, or dissolved in water, its mobile ions can carry charge. Dissolution is not guaranteed for every ionic compound.

Use the metal/nonmetal pattern as a clue, not a definition. Ionic substances can contain polyatomic ions. For example, ammonium chloride contains NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, with covalent N–H bonds inside NH₄⁺.

Guided drill 2 · Balance charges

Calcium forms Ca²⁺ and oxygen forms O²⁻. What is the simplest neutral formula? Explain why the charges are not written as subscripts.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

CaO: one +2 ion balances one −2 ion. Superscripts describe charge; subscripts describe the number or ratio of particles. Ca₂O₂ is not the simplest formula-unit ratio.

3. Covalent bonding and Lewis structures

A covalent bond involves shared electron density attracted to both bonded nuclei. In a Lewis structure, a line represents a shared pair of electrons. A lone pair is a pair not used in a bond.

BondShared pairsElectrons in the bond
Single12
Double24
Triple36

A shared pair counts toward the shell of each bonded atom, but it is counted once in the molecule’s total electron budget.

A repeatable drawing routine

  1. Total: add valence electrons, adjusting for overall charge.
  2. Connect: use the given skeleton or choose a plausible central atom; H is terminal. Join atoms with single bonds.
  3. Subtract: each single bond uses two electrons from the total.
  4. Distribute: complete terminal-atom shells, then place remaining electrons on the central atom. Stop H at two.
  5. Repair: if the central atom lacks an octet, consider turning a neighboring lone pair into a multiple bond.
  6. Check: total electrons, shell counts, and overall charge. Formal charges provide an additional check.
Worked example 2

Build H₂O: eight electrons in the entire structure

  1. Total: 2(1) + 6 = 8 valence electrons.
  2. Connect: put O between two H atoms. Two O–H bonds use 4 electrons.
  3. Place the remaining 4: two lone pairs on O.
  4. Check: 4 bonding + 4 nonbonding = 8 electrons total. Each H counts 2; O counts 8 around it.

Each green pair of dots is one lone pair. The bent layout anticipates the shape discussion below; a Lewis drawing alone is not a full three-dimensional model.

Worked example 3

Build CO₂: why two double bonds are needed

  1. Total: 4 + 2(6) = 16 valence electrons.
  2. Start: use O–C–O. The two single bonds use 4 electrons.
  3. Complete terminal O octets: place the remaining 12 electrons as three lone pairs on each O. Carbon still counts only 4 electrons around it.
  4. Convert: move one lone pair from each O into an additional shared pair with C. No electrons are added.
  5. Check: O=C=O has 8 bonding electrons and 8 lone-pair electrons. Each atom has an octet; each O retains two lone pairs.

Guided drill 3 · Build NH₃

Nitrogen contributes 5 electrons and each H contributes 1. Draw NH₃. State its total electrons, number of N–H bonds, and nitrogen lone pairs.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Total = 5 + 3(1) = 8. Three single bonds use 6 electrons; the remaining 2 form one lone pair on N. N has an octet and each H has a duet.

This flat drawing records connectivity and electrons; actual NH₃ is trigonal pyramidal, not flat.

4. Separate bond polarity from molecular polarity

Electronegativity describes an atom’s ability to attract shared electron density in a bond. Identical atoms share equally in a simple homonuclear bond such as H–H. In H–Cl, Cl attracts the shared density more strongly, giving H a partial positive charge (δ⁺) and Cl a partial negative charge (δ⁻).

Hδ⁺—Clδ⁻
Partial charges are not the full charges H⁺ and Cl⁻.

A larger electronegativity difference usually suggests a more polar bond. Ionic and covalent descriptions are limiting models along a continuum; one numerical cutoff should not be treated as an exception-free law.

First find shape with VSEPR

In the valence-shell electron-pair repulsion model, regions of electron density around a central atom arrange to reduce repulsion. Count each single, double, or triple bond as one region; each lone pair is also one region.

Electron-region geometry includes lone pairs; molecular shape names the atom arrangement

Worked example 4

Compare H₂O with CO₂

  1. Identify polar bonds: O attracts shared density more strongly than either H or C.
  2. Use the full structure: H₂O has four electron regions at O and is bent; CO₂ has two regions at C and is linear.
  3. Combine directions: water’s bond dipoles do not cancel. CO₂’s equal bond dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel.
  4. Conclude: H₂O is polar; CO₂ is nonpolar even though both C=O bonds are polar.

Guided drill 4 · Find the counting error

A student counts four electron regions around carbon in CO₂ because it has two double bonds. What should the count and shape be?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Each double bond counts as one region. Carbon therefore has two regions, giving a linear arrangement. Count directions of electron density, not the number of shared pairs.

5. Metallic bonding and coordinate bond formation

Metallic bonding: electrons extend through the solid

A simple model describes positive metal cores held together by attraction to delocalized valence electrons. These electrons are not confined to one atom or one atom pair. Their response to an electric field explains conduction; bonding can persist as layers shift, helping explain malleability.

Do not picture a directed current continually flowing in an unconnected metal. A net current requires an applied driving influence such as an electric field. Also distinguish solid copper’s mobile electrons from molten NaCl’s mobile ions.

Coordinate bonding: where did the shared pair come from?

A coordinate covalent bond is formed when one partner supplies both electrons of a shared pair. In NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺, nitrogen supplies its lone pair to the proton. The pair is shared in the resulting N–H bond.

Worked example 5

Follow the electron budget into NH₄⁺

  1. Start with NH₃: three N–H bonds and one lone pair on N; 8 valence electrons total.
  2. Add H⁺: it contributes no electron. The nitrogen lone pair becomes the new N–H bonding pair.
  3. Check the product total: 5 + 4(1) − 1 = 8 electrons, all in four bonds.
  4. Show the charge: put brackets around the Lewis structure and write + outside.

No lone pair remains on N. In isolated NH₄⁺, all four N–H bonds are equivalent and the shape is tetrahedral. “Coordinate” describes the pair’s origin during formation; it does not mean the new bond stays a permanently different type.

The flat cross is a Lewis drawing, not a square-planar shape.

A short formal-charge check

For these line-and-dot structures:

Formal charge = valence electrons − nonbonding electrons − bond lines

Count a double bond as two lines and a triple bond as three. For nitrogen in NH₄⁺: 5 − 0 − 4 = +1. Each H has 1 − 0 − 1 = 0, so the sum is +1. Formal charge is electron bookkeeping, not the same thing as partial charge or a measured charge distribution.

Guided drill 5 · Track a donated pair

H₂O uses a lone pair to bind H⁺, forming H₃O⁺. How many O–H bonds and lone pairs remain on O?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

H₂O starts with two bonds and two lone pairs. One lone pair becomes the third O–H bond, leaving three bonds and one lone pair. Total valence electrons: 6 + 3(1) − 1 = 8. Oxygen’s formal charge is 6 − 2 − 3 = +1.

6. Compare bond length and bond energy carefully

Bond length is the equilibrium distance between bonded nuclei. Bond dissociation energy is the energy needed to break a specified bond in the gas phase; average bond energies summarize values across molecular environments.

For comparable bonds between the same elements, increasing bond order generally shortens and strengthens the bond. Thus the usual carbon–carbon comparison is:

Length: C–C > C=C > C≡C
Energy to break: C–C < C=C < C≡C

This is not a universal ranking across different element pairs or every molecular environment. Bond polarity alone also does not establish which bond is strongest.

Worked example 6

Read a bond-energy account

Suppose breaking reactant bonds requires 900 kJ and forming product bonds releases 1,120 kJ, for the stated amount of reaction.

  1. Energy required: +900 kJ.
  2. Energy released: −1,120 kJ.
  3. Net: 900 − 1,120 = −220 kJ.

The reaction releases 220 kJ overall. It does so because bond formation releases more than bond breaking requires—not because breaking bonds releases energy.

These are illustrative totals, not tabulated data for a particular reaction.

Guided drill 6 · Explain the trend

Which carbon–carbon bond is normally shortest: C–C, C=C, or C≡C? Does that mean it requires the least energy to break?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

C≡C is normally shortest in this comparison. It generally requires the most energy to break. Shorter does not mean easier to break.

A bond inside a molecule is different from an attraction between molecules. Boiling water primarily overcomes intermolecular attractions; it does not normally split H₂O into H and O atoms by breaking every O–H covalent bond.

Write your answer and a reason before opening the explanation. For structures, show all lone pairs and any overall charge.

A. Identify and explain · Items 1–4

  1. Classify H–H and H–Cl as nonpolar or polar covalent; identify which end of H–Cl is δ⁻.
  2. Name the dominant bonding model in solid MgO and write its ion ratio.
  3. Explain why copper conducts while solid NaCl conducts poorly.
  4. Explain why the statement “Na loses an electron because electron loss releases energy” is incorrect.
Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation
  1. H–H: nonpolar covalent. H–Cl: polar covalent; Cl is δ⁻ because it attracts shared density more strongly.
  2. Ionic: Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ occur in a 1:1 ratio, giving neutral MgO.
  3. Copper has delocalized electrons that can carry charge. Solid NaCl has ions fixed in a lattice; the ions become mobile on melting or dissolution.
  4. Removing an electron from isolated Na costs ionization energy. Stability of solid NaCl requires the complete energy balance, including lattice formation.

  2. Draw N₂. State bond order and lone pairs on each N.
  3. Draw NH₄⁺. State total valence electrons and nitrogen’s formal charge.
  4. Draw H₂O and identify its electron-region geometry and molecular shape.

C. Diagnose and apply · Items 9–12

9 · Polar bonds, nonpolar molecule

A student concludes that CO₂ is polar because its C=O bonds are polar. What information is missing?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

The student must consider geometry and dipole cancellation. CO₂ is linear with two equal opposing bond dipoles, so its net dipole is zero.

10 · Find the missing electrons

A drawing of H₂O shows only H–O–H bonds, with no dots on oxygen. How many electrons have been omitted?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Four electrons are missing: two lone pairs on O. The two bonds show only 4 of the 8 valence electrons. Without the lone pairs, the drawing is incomplete and misleading for shape prediction.

11 · Keep the comparison valid

A student says, “Every triple bond is stronger than every double bond, whatever atoms are involved.” How should this claim be revised?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Compare similar bonds between the same elements and consider molecular environment. Increasing bond order generally increases bond strength for such comparisons. Bond order alone does not rank all different element pairs.

12 · Determine the net energy change

For a stated reaction amount, breaking bonds requires 1,400 kJ and forming bonds releases 1,050 kJ. Calculate the net change and state the direction of energy transfer.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Net change = 1,400 − 1,050 = +350 kJ. The reaction absorbs 350 kJ overall because bond breaking requires more than bond formation releases.

8. What to remember

  • Ionic: opposite ions attract; charge balance sets the formula-unit ratio.
  • Covalent: shared pairs and lone pairs must fit the electron budget.
  • Metallic: delocalized electrons help explain conduction and malleability.
  • Coordinate formation: one partner supplies both electrons of a new shared pair.
  • Polarity: combine bond polarity with molecular shape.
  • Energy: breaking bonds requires energy; forming bonds releases energy.
