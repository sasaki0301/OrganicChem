Phenols and Naming Phenols

Identify an OH group attached directly to an aromatic ring, compare phenol with benzyl alcohol, and practice numbering and naming substituted phenols.

1. What is a phenol?

A phenol has a hydroxyl group, –OH, bonded directly to a carbon of an aromatic ring. The simplest example, phenol, is C₆H₅OH.

The left structural formula shows one Kekulé resonance contributor; the right skeletal formula uses a circle to represent the delocalized benzene system. OH is explicitly shown in both.

Compare the attachment · Phenol and benzyl alcohol

Phenol: C₆H₅–OH. Oxygen is directly bonded to a ring carbon.

Benzyl alcohol: C₆H₅–CH₂–OH. A CH₂ group lies between the ring and OH. This is an alcohol, not a phenol.

The diagrams show benzyl alcohol. The label CH₂OH is a condensed side group: the ring is bonded to the CH₂ carbon, which is bonded to OH.

This lesson names simple substituted benzenes using phenol as the parent when OH is the principal functional group. More complex compounds, including those with higher-priority groups, can require a different parent or a hydroxy prefix.

2. Phenol and ethanol: acidity

Phenol can donate H⁺ from O–H, forming the phenoxide ion:

C₆H₅OH ⇌ C₆H₅O⁻ + H⁺

Phenol is a weak acid but is more acidic than ethanol in the usual comparison. In phenoxide, the negative charge is delocalized between oxygen and positions on the ring through resonance. The ethoxide ion, CH₃CH₂O⁻, lacks the same aromatic resonance stabilization.

Resonance contributors describe one delocalized ion; the atoms do not move between structures. Substituents and solvent can change acidity, so compare specified compounds under stated conditions.

Overview · Benzenediols

A benzene ring with two hydroxyl groups attached directly to the ring is a benzenediol, also called a dihydroxybenzene or a dihydric phenol. The suffix -diol indicates two OH groups. Number the ring to give the OH groups the lowest possible locants.

The three constitutional isomers all have molecular formula C₆H₆O₂, but their OH groups occupy different positions on the ring:

Benzene-1,2-diol · catechol (pyrocatechol)

The two OH groups are on adjacent carbons: 1,2, or ortho.

Benzene-1,3-diol · resorcinol

The two OH groups are separated by one ring carbon: 1,3, or meta.

Benzene-1,4-diol · hydroquinone

The two OH groups are on opposite ring carbons: 1,4, or para.

The ring numbers identify the OH positions; the circle in each skeletal formula represents the delocalized aromatic system. These drawings show the same connectivity in two conventions.

Quick check · Compare isomers
If the OH groups are opposite each other, the compound is benzene-1,4-diol (hydroquinone). The other two arrangements are 1,2 (catechol) and 1,3 (resorcinol).

3. Naming simple substituted phenols

  1. Choose phenol as the parent for the simple examples shown.
  2. Assign carbon 1 to the ring carbon bonded to OH.
  3. Try both directions around the ring and choose the lowest sorted set of substituent locants, comparing at the first difference.
  4. List different substituents alphabetically, with their locants. If the full locant sets tie, assign the lower number to the alphabetically first substituent at the first point of difference.

In disubstituted cases, the relationship to OH can also be described as ortho (1,2), meta (1,3), or para (1,4). These prefixes describe relative positions; numbers state them explicitly.

4. Worked examples

Example 1 · One ring substituent

Condensed: HOC₆H₄CH₃, with OH and methyl in a 1,3 relationship.

  1. The OH-bearing carbon is C-1.
  2. Number toward methyl: it is at C-3, not C-5.
  3. Name: 3-methylphenol, also called m-cresol.

The orange numbers show the selected ring numbering. In the skeletal formula, the unlabeled line end is a methyl carbon.

Example 2 · Number first, alphabetize second

Condensed: HOC₆H₃(NO₂)CH₃, with nitro at 2 and methyl at 4.

  1. Set OH at C-1. The shown direction gives substituent locants 2,4; the reverse direction gives 4,6.
  2. Choose 2,4 because 2 is lower at the first difference.
  3. Write the groups alphabetically: methyl before nitro. Name: 4-methyl-2-nitrophenol.

Example 3 · When the locant sets tie

Condensed: HOC₆H₂Br(CH₃)(NO₂), with bromo at 2, methyl at 4 and nitro at 6.

Either direction gives the sorted set 2,4,6. Alphabetically, bromo comes before methyl and nitro, so the direction shown gives bromo the lower locant. Write 2-bromo-4-methyl-6-nitrophenol.

6. Key takeaways

  • Phenols have OH attached directly to an aromatic ring; benzyl alcohol has a CH₂ between ring and OH.
  • Phenoxide is resonance-stabilized, helping explain phenol’s greater acidity than ethanol.
  • In these simple phenol-parent examples, the OH-bearing carbon is C-1.
  • Choose the lowest substituent locant set, then alphabetize the different substituents in the name.
  • Ortho, meta and para describe 1,2, 1,3 and 1,4 relationships.
