Cis–Trans Isomerism in Cycloalkanes

Learn to compare the faces of a ring, read wedge-and-dash drawings, and distinguish stereoisomers from chair conformations.

1. Why a ring can give cis and trans isomers

In an open chain, many single bonds can rotate, though rotation may encounter energy barriers. In a ring, the connected framework restricts independent rotation. Different positions relative to the two faces of the ring can produce distinct stereoisomers: compounds with the same atom-to-atom connectivity but different spatial arrangements.

Compare a specified pair of substituents attached to different ring carbons:

  • cis: both are on the same face—both up or both down.
  • trans: they are on opposite faces—one up and one down.
“Up” and “down” describe opposite faces relative to a ring reference, not the direction of the line on the screen. The ring may be puckered. A flat polygon is a drawing convention, not a claim that every ring is planar.

For the simple disubstituted examples here, the substituents may be identical or different: two methyl groups can give cis and trans forms. A lone methyl substituent has no second specified substituent to compare. Two groups on the same ring carbon are not a cis/trans pair with each other in this introductory description.

2. Read a wedge-and-dash drawing

  • Solid wedge: the bond projects toward the viewer.
  • Hashed or dashed wedge: the bond projects away from the viewer.
  • Ordinary line: the bond is drawn in the reference plane; if the substituent’s stereochemistry is not otherwise given, the drawing does not establish its face.

In these schematic drawings, the ring is our reference plane. Compare the bonds to the specified substituents. Two solid wedges or two dashed wedges show the same face; one of each shows opposite faces. Reversing both wedges changes which face is drawn toward you but preserves the cis/trans relationship.

Same face: cis-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane

Both methyl groups have solid wedges. They are on the same face, so the relationship is cis. Both representations show the same connectivity and wedge information.

Opposite faces: trans-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane

One methyl bond is a solid wedge and the other is a dashed wedge: the groups are on opposite faces, so the relationship is trans.

When the drawing does not tell you

The ordinary bonds show two methyl groups on a ring, but their faces are not specified. You can name the connectivity 1,3-dimethylcyclohexane, but cannot determine cis or trans from this drawing.

3. Name the structure

  1. Count the ring carbons and choose the parent.
  2. Identify the substituents and number the ring to give the lowest locants, using alphabetical order to break a complete tie where appropriate.
  3. Compare the faces of the specified pair of groups using their wedges.
  4. Add cis- or trans- before the connectivity name.

Worked example · Both groups behind the page

There are five ring carbons and two methyl groups at positions 1 and 3. Both substituent bonds are hashed, so they point to the same face. The name is cis-1,3-dimethylcyclopentane.

Cis/trans gives a relative relationship. Some substituted rings also have stereochemistry that is more fully described with additional stereochemical descriptors; this lesson concentrates on cis/trans.

4. Chair flips: up/down versus axial/equatorial

Cyclohexane commonly adopts a nonplanar chair. Axial and equatorial describe bond positions in a particular chair; up and down describe the ring face.

Follow the same two groups through a chair flip

GroupBefore flipAfter flipFace
Group 1axial upequatorial upup → up
Group 2equatorial upaxial upup → up

Both groups remain on the same face, so the relationship remains cis. Axial and equatorial interchange; up and down do not. A chair flip changes conformation, not cis/trans identity.

For example, a cis-1,2-disubstituted cyclohexane has one axial and one equatorial substituent in each chair; a flip swaps their positions. Do not equate “axial” with up or “equatorial” with down: either orientation can be up or down depending on the ring carbon and chair.

Show answer
No. The second chair is another conformation of the same cis compound. Axial and equatorial positions swap while each substituent retains its up or down orientation.

6. Key takeaways

  • Cis means the selected substituents share a face; trans means opposite faces.
  • The selected substituents can be identical or different and, in the examples here, are on different ring carbons.
  • Solid and dashed wedges give the three-dimensional information needed; an unspecified bond may leave the answer undetermined.
  • A cyclohexane ring flip swaps axial and equatorial positions while preserving up/down and cis/trans.
