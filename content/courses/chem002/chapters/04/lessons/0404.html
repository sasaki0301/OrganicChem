1. From an open chain to a ring

A cycloalkane is a saturated hydrocarbon with carbon atoms connected in one or more rings. In this lesson we focus on single-ring cycloalkanes with only single bonds. The prefix cyclo- signals the ring.

One compound, two ways to draw it

These drawings both show cyclopentane. Follow the five C–C bonds until the chain closes back on itself.

The semi-displayed structure groups hydrogens with each carbon as CH₂. In the skeletal drawing, every unlabeled corner is carbon and its attached hydrogens are implied. A flat polygon shows connectivity; it does not necessarily show the real three-dimensional shape.

For a saturated hydrocarbon containing one ring, the molecular formula is CnH2n. For the same number of carbons, an acyclic alkane is CnH2n+2. Closing one ring removes two hydrogens. These formulas do not apply unchanged to compounds with additional rings or multiple bonds.

2. Naming simple substituted rings

  1. Choose the parent. In the simple cases here, compare the number of carbons in the ring with those in the attached acyclic chain. Use the ring as parent when it has at least as many carbons as that chain; if the chain has more, use the chain as parent and name the ring as a cycloalkyl substituent.
  2. Find and name each substituent. A line ending at an unlabeled point outside the ring adds one carbon: a methyl group. A two-carbon path is an ethyl group.
  3. Number the ring. For one substituent on a simple ring, position 1 is understood and is omitted. For several, compare possible starting positions and directions; choose the lowest set of locants at the first difference.
  4. Resolve a complete tie. If numbering choices produce the same locant set, give the lower number to the substituent cited first alphabetically. Alphabetical order does not override a lower set of locants.
  5. Assemble. List different substituents alphabetically; use di-, tri-, and so on for repeated identical groups, with one locant per group. Ignore these multiplying prefixes when alphabetizing.
These are introductory rules for simple monocyclic hydrocarbons and simple side chains. More complex parent structures and functional groups require additional rules. The plain bond lines in this lesson specify connectivity only: unless wedges or dashes are given, do not infer cis or trans.

3. Worked naming examples

Example 1 · One substituent

Methylcyclohexane

  1. The ring has six carbons, so the parent is cyclohexane.
  2. The extra end outside the ring is one carbon: methyl.
  3. With one substituent its position is understood: methylcyclohexane.

In the structural drawing, the substituted ring carbon is CH; each of the other five is CH₂, and the branch is CH₃. Molecular formula: C₇H₁₄.

Example 2 · Two different substituents

Name the five-carbon ring

  1. The ring has five carbons: cyclopentane. The branches are ethyl (two carbons) and methyl (one carbon).
  2. Number to obtain 1,3 rather than 1,4. Either group can have 1 and still give 1,3.
  3. The locant set ties, so ethyl, which comes before methyl alphabetically, receives 1.

Connectivity name: 1-ethyl-3-methylcyclopentane. No cis/trans relationship is specified by the plain bonds.

Example 3 · Same carbon and first difference

Three substituents on a ring

  1. The parent is cyclohexane. One ring carbon bears two methyl groups, and another bears an ethyl group.
  2. Numbering from the carbon with two methyl groups toward ethyl gives 1,1,3; starting at ethyl can give 1,3,3. At the second locant, 1 is less than 3.
  3. Use 1,1 for the two methyl groups; list ethyl before methyl while keeping their locants.

Connectivity name: 3-ethyl-1,1-dimethylcyclohexane. This example shows that alphabetical order does not decide the numbering when the locant sets differ.

Example 4 · Choosing the parent

When an attached chain is longer

Attach a straight four-carbon chain to a cyclopropane ring. The chain has four carbons, compared with three in the ring, so the chain is the parent and the ring is a cyclopropyl substituent. Attachment at an end gives 1-cyclopropylbutane. The ring carbon at the attachment point is part of the ring, not an extra carbon in the chain.

This example concerns a simple ring attached to a simple acyclic chain.

4. Ring shape and strain

A ring limits rotation around its bonds. Angle strain comes from bond angles departing from the preferred tetrahedral value; torsional strain can arise when neighboring bonds eclipse. Steric interactions can also contribute.

Cyclopropane

Its ring angles are approximately 60°, far from the tetrahedral angle; eclipsed bonds also contribute to strain.

Cyclobutane and cyclohexane

Cyclobutane is puckered, not a perfectly flat square, and is strained. Cyclohexane can adopt a nonplanar chair conformation with very low ring strain.

A skeletal polygon is a convenient map of which atoms are connected; it should not be read as a precise picture of bond angles or a planar conformation.

6. Key takeaways

  • Single-ring saturated hydrocarbons have molecular formula CnH2n.
  • Count ring vertices, identify substituents, and select the ring or chain parent for the simple cases studied.
  • Compare locant sets first; use alphabetical order to resolve a complete numbering tie and to order different substituent names.
  • Plain skeletal and semi-displayed diagrams show connections; they do not establish cis/trans geometry.
  • Ring strain includes angle and torsional effects; cyclohexane can adopt a low-strain chair.
