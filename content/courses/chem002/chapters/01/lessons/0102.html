Lesson 2: Organic vs Inorganic Compounds

Sugar and salt can both dissolve in water. Does that make them the same kind of compound? In this lesson, use composition, bonding, and particle behavior to explain their similarities and differences.

Allow about 30–40 minutes. Prepare paper for your answers.

What you will learn

  • Classify familiar compounds and explain carbon-containing exceptions.
  • Distinguish organic/inorganic classification from ionic/covalent bonding.
  • Explain why solubility and conductivity depend on particles and structure.
  • Use property evidence carefully without treating a trend as a universal rule.

Connection to Lesson 1: Organic does not mean “from a living thing,” and a C–H bond is not required in every organic compound. We will now apply these ideas to familiar substances.

Study routine: Read the worked example → answer the nearby drill → reveal the explanation → correct your reasoning.

1. Classify the compound, then explain why

Organic compounds are carbon compounds belonging to the families studied in organic chemistry, such as hydrocarbons, alcohols, sugars, and organic acids. Their structures commonly contain C–H bonds, but some do not.

Inorganic compounds include water, many salts and minerals, and certain carbon-containing families traditionally treated as inorganic. Familiar exceptions include carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, carbonates, and hydrogen carbonates.

A useful sequence—not a perfect formula test
  1. Identify the substance. Use its name and formula. Decide whether the sample is a compound or a mixture.
  2. Check for carbon. A compound without carbon is inorganic.
  3. If carbon is present, check the family or exception. Do not decide from C–H bonds, source, or one physical property alone.
Worked example 1 · Compare two carbon compounds

Acetic acid and sodium hydrogen carbonate

Acetic acid, CH3COOH: Identify the substance → recognize an organic acid → classify it as organic.

Sodium hydrogen carbonate, NaHCO3: Identify the hydrogen carbonate family → recognize a conventional exception → classify it as inorganic.

Reasoning: Both contain C, H, and O. Those element symbols alone do not place them in the same category. In NaHCO3, hydrogen is bonded to oxygen, not carbon.

Worked example 2 · Avoid the C–H shortcut

Urea, H2N–C(=O)–NH2

  1. Locate the carbon: it is bonded to oxygen and two nitrogens.
  2. Locate the hydrogens: they are attached to nitrogen.
  3. Recall the compound: urea is organic even though it has no C–H bond.

Conclusion: “No C–H bond” is not a sufficient reason to classify a compound as inorganic.

2. Classification and bonding answer different questions

Organic or inorganic? asks which chemical category a compound belongs to. Ionic or covalent? asks how its particles are held together. These two comparisons are related, but they are not interchangeable.

Covalent bonding

Atoms share electron pairs. Many familiar organic compounds consist of molecules with covalent bonds.

Methane: CH4 is organic and molecular. The drawing shows connectivity; the actual molecule is tetrahedral.

Ionic bonding

Oppositely charged ions attract one another. In a solid salt, the ions form an extended crystal lattice.

Sodium chloride: NaCl is inorganic and ionic. This is a schematic slice of a three-dimensional crystal. NaCl gives the 1:1 ratio of ions, not a separate molecule in the solid.

Water is a useful counterexample: H2O is inorganic, but its O–H bonds are covalent. Being inorganic does not require ionic bonding.

Explore an organic saltHide the organic salt example

Sodium acetate, CH3COONa, is a salt of the organic acid acetic acid. It contains Na+ ions and CH3COO− ions. Ionic attractions act between ions; covalent bonds hold the atoms together within each acetate ion. Organic chemistry therefore also includes compounds that contain ions.

3. Compare properties without overgeneralizing

Properties depend on bonding, particle arrangement, and attractions between particles. The following are patterns to explain, not tests that classify every compound.

Why do sugar and oil behave differently in water?

Water is polar: its electrical charge is unevenly distributed. Sucrose has many O–H groups that can interact strongly with water. Oil molecules contain large nonpolar hydrocarbon regions, so oils do not mix well with water.

The phrase “like dissolves like” is a useful first guide: substances tend to mix more readily when their intermolecular interactions are compatible. It is not an absolute rule; molecule size, structure, temperature, and the particular solvent matter.

Worked example 3 · Explain the observation

Sugar dissolves in water, but cooking oil forms a layer

  1. Identify the substances: sucrose is organic; cooking oil is a mixture mainly of organic compounds.
  2. Consider interactions: sucrose interacts favorably with water; the large nonpolar regions of oil molecules do not.
  3. Conclude: the different behavior follows from molecular structure and interactions, not from one being organic and the other inorganic.

No. Both sucrose (organic) and sodium chloride (inorganic) dissolve in water. The observation establishes that the sample dissolves under those conditions, not its category. Its chemical identity, composition, or structure is needed for a justified classification.

4. Dissolving is not the same as forming ions

Electrical conduction requires mobile charged particles. In solutions, these are usually ions. A substance may dissolve as neutral molecules or produce separated ions; these lead to different electrical behavior.

Solid sodium chloride

Ions are present, but held in lattice positions. They cannot move freely through the solid.

Result: no appreciable ionic conduction.

Salt dissolved in water

Na+ and Cl− ions separate and move through the solution.

Result: the solution conducts.

Sugar dissolved in water

Neutral sucrose molecules disperse without producing appreciable ions.

Result: very poor conductivity.

Schematic particle diagrams: labels identify whole particles; sizes and spacing are not to scale. Water molecules are omitted from the solution diagrams, and each ion is actually surrounded by water.

Salt: NaCl(s) → Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq)

Sugar: C12H22O11(s) → C12H22O11(aq)

Here, (s) means solid and (aq) means dissolved in water. Compare the charges: the salt produces mobile ions; the dissolved sugar remains neutral.

Worked example 4 · Link particles to conductivity

Two clear solutions, different conductivity

Solution A contains dissolved NaCl. Solution B contains dissolved sucrose.

  1. Name the dissolved particles: A contains Na+ and Cl−; B contains sucrose molecules.
  2. Check charge and mobility: A has mobile ions. B has mobile molecules, but these molecules are neutral.
  3. Explain: NaCl solution conducts much better. Dissolving alone does not guarantee electrical conductivity.

Pure water itself has a very small concentration of ions, so “very poor conductivity” is more accurate than an absolute claim of zero conductivity.

Can an organic compound produce a conducting solution?

Yes. Acetic acid reacts partly with water to produce ions. An electrolyte yields ions in solution; a nonelectrolyte, such as sucrose, dissolves without appreciably producing ions.

Acetic acid is a weak electrolyte because only a fraction of its molecules ionize. Dissolved NaCl is a strong electrolyte because it is present essentially as separated ions in dilute water solution. “Weak” and “strong” describe the extent of ion production, not whether a solution is dilute or concentrated.

See how acetic acid produces ionsHide the equation

CH3COOH + H2O ⇌ CH3COO− + H3O+

Acetate ions and hydronium ions form. The double arrow indicates that the reaction can occur in both directions; much of the acid remains as neutral molecules. You only need to recognize that ions form at this stage.

The explanation overlooks mobility. In the solid, ions occupy lattice positions. When NaCl melts, the ions can move and carry charge, so the molten salt conducts. This explanation concerns particle behavior, not the organic/inorganic label.

6. What to remember

  • Classify by chemical identity and family. Carbon-containing exceptions exist; C–H bonds are common but not required in every organic compound.
  • Separate category from bonding. Inorganic compounds can be covalent; organic salts can contain ions.
  • Explain solubility through interactions. Both organic and inorganic compounds include water-soluble and poorly soluble examples.
  • Explain conduction through mobile charge. Ions locked in a solid lattice differ from ions free to move in solution or a melt.
  • Use patterns cautiously. No single observation of state, solubility, conductivity, or flammability reliably classifies every compound.

Key terms

TermMeaning
Organic compoundA carbon compound within the families studied in organic chemistry; conventional inorganic carbon families are excluded.
Inorganic compoundA compound outside the organic category, including many salts, minerals, water, and certain carbon compounds.
Covalent bondA bond involving shared electron pairs between atoms.
Ionic bondElectrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. Electron transfer can form ions; the attraction between them is the bond.
Crystal latticeAn ordered, repeating arrangement of particles in a crystalline solid.
SolubilityThe maximum amount or concentration of a solute that can dissolve in a specified solvent under stated conditions at equilibrium.
ElectrolyteA substance that yields ions when dissolved, allowing the solution to conduct electricity.
NonelectrolyteA substance that dissolves without appreciably producing ions.
