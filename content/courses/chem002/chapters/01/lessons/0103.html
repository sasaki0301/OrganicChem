Lesson 3: Applications of Organic Chemistry in Daily Life

A plate of rice, an LPG stove, a medicine tablet, and a plastic container all involve organic chemistry. The useful question is not only “Does it contain carbon?” but also “What does the compound do, and why?”

Allow about 30–40 minutes. Use paper for the drills and practice.

GoalsMedicineFuelsFoodMaterialsAgriculturePracticeSummary

What you will learn

  • Identify organic compounds used in medicine, fuels, food, materials, and agriculture.
  • Connect a compound’s properties or reactions to its everyday function.
  • Distinguish a pure compound from a product containing several substances.
  • Evaluate claims such as “natural means safe” and “plant-based means biodegradable.”
Use this four-part explanation:
  1. Compound: What substance is involved?
  2. Property or reaction: What chemical feature matters?
  3. Application: How does that feature serve the purpose?
  4. Limit: What should we avoid assuming?

This builds on Lessons 1 and 2: organic/inorganic classification does not by itself determine safety, solubility, or usefulness. Names are provided here; systematic naming will be developed in later lessons.

1. Medicine: chemical structure affects biological activity

Many active ingredients in medicines are organic compounds. Examples include paracetamol, C8H9NO2, and aspirin, C9H8O4. A medicine product may contain an active ingredient together with other ingredients used to make the tablet, capsule, or liquid.

Organic chemistry helps researchers prepare molecules and study how their shape, charge, and chemical groups affect interactions with biological targets. Changes in structure can change activity, absorption, or breakdown. A useful medicine must also be evaluated for effectiveness and safety.

Worked example 1

Must a medicine be organic?

Compare paracetamol, an organic active ingredient used for pain and fever, with calcium carbonate, CaCO3, an inorganic ingredient used in some antacids.

  1. Identify: these are different compounds with different actions.
  2. Explain: calcium carbonate reacts with acid, which explains its antacid use. It does not need to be organic to act in the body.
  3. Conclude: chemical behavior—not the organic label alone—determines whether a substance can serve a medicinal purpose.

Guided drill 1 · Correct the reasoning

A student says, “The body contains organic molecules, so every medicine must be organic to interact with it.” Identify the error and give a counterexample.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

The body also contains water and inorganic ions, and interactions depend on chemical properties. Calcium carbonate is inorganic yet is used in some antacids because it reacts with acid. Medicines do not have to be organic.

The examples explain chemical applications; they are not instructions for choosing or taking a medicine.

2. Fuels: reactions transfer chemical energy

Hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen. Methane, CH4, and propane, C3H8, are examples. LPG commonly contains propane and butane; gasoline and kerosene are mixtures of many hydrocarbons, not single compounds with one formula.

During combustion, fuel reacts with oxygen. For complete combustion of a hydrocarbon, the products are carbon dioxide and water. The overall reaction releases energy that can be transferred as heat.

CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O
Complete combustion; energy is released overall.

Why energy is released: breaking reactant bonds requires energy, while forming product bonds releases energy. In combustion, the latter release is greater, giving a net energy release.

Worked example 2

Explain why LPG can heat a cooking pot

  1. Compound: propane is one of the hydrocarbons in LPG.
  2. Reaction: propane reacts with oxygen, releasing energy overall.
  3. Application: energy transfers from the flame and hot gases to the pot and its contents.
  4. Limit: combustion is not emission-free. Complete combustion produces CO2; insufficient oxygen can lead to carbon monoxide and soot.

Biofuels: source and emissions are different questions

A biofuel is a fuel produced from biomass—material from living or recently living organisms. Ethanol produced from fermented plant sugars and biodiesel produced from suitable oils or fats are examples.

Bioethanol still releases CO2 when burned. Evaluating its environmental impact also requires considering feedstock production, processing, transport, and land use. A renewable source does not automatically mean zero emissions.

Guided drill 2 · Read the reaction

Using the methane equation, answer: (a) How many oxygen atoms are on each side? (b) Which product contains carbon? (c) Does complete combustion mean that no CO2 is produced?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

(a) Four oxygen atoms on each side: 2O2 gives four; CO2 plus 2H2O also gives four. (b) CO2. (c) No. Carbon dioxide is a product of complete combustion, so complete combustion is not carbon-free combustion.

3. Food: organic and inorganic nutrients work together

Food is a mixture. Carbohydrates, fats, proteins, and vitamins are organic substances, while water and mineral nutrients are inorganic. Not all nutrients are organic, and not all nutrients supply energy.

Worked example 3

Follow starch from a meal to usable building blocks

  1. Identify: rice contains starch, an organic carbohydrate made of many linked glucose units.
  2. Explain the transformation: digestive enzymes help break digestible starch into smaller sugars, ultimately including glucose.
  3. Connect to use: absorbed glucose can participate in cellular reactions that release energy.
  4. State the limit: rice is not pure starch, and digestion is not the same process as burning food in a flame.

Guided drill 3 · Read a drink’s ingredients

A drink contains water, sucrose, and dissolved sodium chloride. Identify the organic compound and the inorganic components. Which listed ingredient is a source of dietary energy?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Organic: sucrose. Inorganic: water and sodium chloride. Sucrose supplies dietary energy. Water and the salt have other physiological roles; being a nutrient does not automatically mean supplying calories.

4. Materials and cleaning: structure explains function

A polymer is a substance made of macromolecules—very large molecules containing many repeated structural units. A monomer is a small molecule that can react to contribute units to a polymer chain or network.

Many familiar polymers are organic: polyethylene in packaging, cellulose in cotton and paper, natural rubber, and nylon. Polymers can be natural or synthetic. The general term “polymer” is broader than organic chemistry; not every polymer has a carbon-based backbone.

Read the polymer scheme

  1. Start with ethene: CH2=CH2 has a C=C double bond.
  2. Connect many units: during polymerization, new bonds join the units into a chain; the polyethylene backbone has C–C single bonds.
  3. Read the brackets: [–CH2–CH2–]n means the unit repeats many times. The symbol n is not one fixed number for every chain.
Worked example 4

Why use polyethylene for packaging?

  1. Structure: polyethylene consists of long hydrocarbon chains.
  2. Properties: it is lightweight and resists water. Chain branching and packing help determine stiffness and flexibility.
  3. Use: different grades can be used for flexible films or more rigid containers.
  4. Trade-off: durability is useful during service, but discarded polyethylene can persist in the environment.

A connection to Lesson 2: how soap helps remove grease

Soap is a surfactant: a substance that acts at interfaces, such as between oil and water. Its nonpolar tail associates with oily material, while its charged head interacts with water. With washing and rinsing, soap helps disperse grease so it can be carried away.

Soap is not simply “making oil become water-soluble molecules.” It helps organize and disperse oily material in water. This is a practical application of molecular regions having different interactions.

Guided drill 4 · Explain using structure

Water alone does not remove a greasy film well. Explain why adding soap can help. Mention both the head and the tail.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

The hydrocarbon tails associate with the grease, while the charged heads interact with water. Soap helps disperse the oily material into the wash water, allowing it to be removed during rinsing. The two regions serve different roles.

5. Agriculture and the environment: usefulness has conditions

Organic chemistry contributes to fertilizers, plant-growth regulators, pest-control products, and materials used in agriculture. These products are not all organic compounds, and their environmental effects depend on their identities and how they are used.

Urea: organic nitrogen source

Urea, CO(NH2)2, contains nitrogen. In soil, it can be converted to ammonium; further transformations can produce nitrate, forms plants can use.

Ammonium sulfate: inorganic nitrogen source

Ammonium sulfate, (NH4)2SO4, is an inorganic salt that supplies ammonium ions. An inorganic compound can also serve an agricultural purpose.

Here, “urea is organic” is a chemical classification. It does not establish whether a fertilizer is allowed under an organic-farming certification system.

Read environmental claims carefully

TermWhat it describesWhat to check

Worked example 5

Evaluate “plant-based means it disappears anywhere”

A cup is described as plant-based and suitable for industrial composting.

  1. Identify the claims: “plant-based” concerns feedstock; “industrial composting” concerns an end-of-life process.
  2. Check the conditions: industrial composting uses controlled conditions that may differ greatly from a garden, beach, or ocean.
  3. Conclude: these claims do not show that the cup rapidly breaks down wherever it is discarded.

Guided drill 5 · Ask for useful evidence

A packaging advertisement says only “100% natural and eco-friendly.” Name two additional pieces of information needed to evaluate its disposal claim.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Possible answers include the exact material, the specified breakdown environment and time, evidence supporting compostability, and whether a local collection or processing facility accepts it. “Natural” alone does not establish an appropriate disposal route.

Keep the categories separate: organic ≠ harmless; natural ≠ automatically safe; biobased ≠ automatically biodegradable. Evaluate the particular substance, exposure, use, and disposal conditions.

6. Product or pure compound?

A student writes, “Gasoline is one organic compound, so it has one molecular formula.” Correct the statement.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Gasoline is a mixture of many compounds, mainly hydrocarbons, and may include additives. Individual components have formulas, but gasoline as a whole does not have one molecular formula.

7. Compare useful materials

Both starch and polyethylene are polymers. Does that mean they must have the same digestibility or environmental behavior? Explain.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

No. “Polymer” describes the presence of macromolecules with repeated units. Their chemical structures and bonds differ. Human digestive enzymes can act on digestible starch, but do not digest polyethylene in the same way. Shared membership in a broad category does not imply identical behavior.

8. Evaluate a fuel claim

A poster states, “Bioethanol comes from plants, so burning it releases no carbon dioxide.” Correct the claim and identify one other stage to consider when evaluating environmental impact.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Burning bioethanol releases CO2. Its biological origin does not eliminate combustion emissions. Relevant additional stages include growing the feedstock, processing it into fuel, transporting it, and changes in land use.

7. What to remember

  • Medicine: molecular structure and chemical behavior help explain activity. Some medicines contain inorganic active ingredients.
  • Energy: organic fuels can release energy through combustion; their source and emissions must be considered separately.
  • Food: organic nutrients work alongside inorganic water and minerals.
  • Materials: polymer structure affects properties; surfactants combine regions that interact differently with oil and water.
  • Agriculture and environment: useful applications require attention to conditions, effects, and disposal—not just labels.
