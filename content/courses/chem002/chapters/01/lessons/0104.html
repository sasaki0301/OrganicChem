Lesson 1 recap · Definition and history

  • Scope: organic chemistry studies organic compounds—their structures, properties, preparation, and reactions.
  • Classification: organic compounds contain carbon, but some carbon compounds are conventionally inorganic. C–H bonds are common, not required.
  • History: vitalism proposed that making organic compounds required a special force associated with living organisms.
  • Evidence: in 1828, Friedrich Wöhler prepared urea from ammonium cyanate without a living organism. This challenged vitalism; its rejection developed gradually.
  • Structure: a molecular formula gives atom counts. A structural formula shows connections, which help explain why substances behave differently.
Remember the counterexamples: CO2 contains carbon but is inorganic. Urea, H2N–C(=O)–NH2, is organic but has no C–H bond: its hydrogens are bonded to nitrogen.

Lesson 2 recap · Classification and properties

Keep two questions separate: “Organic or inorganic?” concerns chemical category. “Ionic or covalent?” concerns bonding.

Three examples that separate category from bonding
CompoundCategoryParticle description
Methane, CH4OrganicMolecules with covalent bonds
Water, H2OInorganicMolecules with covalent bonds
Sodium chloride, NaClInorganicIons in a crystal lattice when solid
  • Solubility: both organic and inorganic compounds include water-soluble and poorly soluble examples. Consider interactions with the solvent.
  • Conductivity: charged particles must be able to move. Solid NaCl does not conduct appreciably; dissolved or molten NaCl conducts.
  • Dissolving: sucrose dissolves as neutral molecules, so its solution conducts very poorly. Acetic acid is organic but forms some ions in water.
  • Limits: state, flammability, solubility, or conductivity alone cannot reliably classify every compound.

One example connecting all three lessons

A drink contains sucrose, sodium chloride, and water.

  1. Identify: sucrose is organic; sodium chloride and water are inorganic. The drink is a mixture.
  2. Explain: dissolved salt supplies mobile ions; dissolved sucrose remains mainly as neutral molecules.
  3. Connect to use: sucrose supplies dietary energy; water and the salt have other roles. The whole product cannot be treated as one organic compound.

Misconception check · Replace the shortcut

Avoid this statementUse this more accurate idea
“Every carbon compound is organic.”CO, CO2, carbonates, and hydrogen carbonates are familiar inorganic exceptions.
“An organic compound must contain a C–H bond.”Urea is an organic counterexample.
“Inorganic means ionic.”Water and CO2 are inorganic molecular compounds with covalent bonds.
“If it dissolves, it conducts well.”Dissolving may disperse neutral molecules rather than ions, as with sucrose.
“Wöhler immediately ended vitalism.”His experiment provided important evidence; scientific acceptance changed over time.
“Plant-based plastic breaks down anywhere.”Feedstock and breakdown behavior are different questions. Check the material and required conditions.

Mixed practice · Retrieve, explain, apply

Answer on paper before opening each explanation. These are review questions; the chapter assessment is opened separately at the end.

1. Classify with reasons

Classify ethanol (CH3CH2OH), calcium carbonate (CaCO3), and urea [CO(NH2)2]. Give a reason for each.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Ethanol: organic; it belongs to the alcohol family. Calcium carbonate: inorganic; carbonates are a conventional carbon-containing exception. Urea: organic despite lacking C–H bonds. Carbon or hydrogen symbols alone are not a complete classification test.

2. Explain the historical evidence

Why did making urea from ammonium cyanate challenge vitalism more directly than extracting urea from urine?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Extraction only separates a substance already produced by an organism. Preparing urea from an inorganic starting material without a living organism challenged the proposed need for a vital force. It did not demonstrate that every organic compound could already be made in a laboratory.

3. Interpret structural information

Ethanol, CH3–CH2–OH, and dimethyl ether, CH3–O–CH3, both have molecular formula C2H6O. Explain why the shared formula does not make them the same compound.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

The numbers of atoms match, but the connections differ. In ethanol, oxygen is bonded to carbon and hydrogen; in dimethyl ether, it is bonded to two carbons. A molecular formula does not specify all structural information.

4. Reason from observations

Two known samples—sucrose and NaCl—both dissolve in water. One solution conducts much better. Identify it and explain using particles. Why would conductivity alone be insufficient to identify a completely unknown substance?

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

The NaCl solution conducts much better because it contains mobile Na+ and Cl− ions. Sucrose dissolves as neutral molecules. For an unknown, many different substances can produce mobile ions; conductivity neither uniquely identifies the compound nor determines whether it is organic.

5. Correct two application claims

Give one counterexample to each: (a) all medicines have organic active ingredients; (b) all nutrients are organic and provide energy.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

(a) Calcium carbonate is an inorganic active ingredient in some antacids. (b) Water and minerals are inorganic nutrients that do not supply dietary energy. Vitamins are organic nutrients, but they also do not supply energy in the way carbohydrates and fats do.

6. Connect fuel chemistry and emissions

A student says, “A plant-derived fuel cannot release carbon dioxide.” Correct the claim. Then state why combustion releases energy overall.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

Bioethanol releases CO2 when burned; biological origin does not eliminate combustion emissions. Combustion releases energy overall because forming the product bonds releases more energy than is needed to break the reactant bonds.

7. Apply structure to function

Explain how the two regions of a soap ion help remove grease from a plate.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

The nonpolar hydrocarbon tail associates with grease, while the charged head interacts with water. Soap helps disperse oily material into the wash water so it can be rinsed away. The explanation depends on both regions, not simply the label “organic.”

8. Evaluate an environmental claim

A package is labelled “plant-based; industrially compostable.” Does this establish rapid breakdown in a home compost pile or the sea? State what still needs checking.

Show answer and explanationHide answer and explanation

No. The feedstock label does not establish breakdown behavior, and industrial composting conditions differ from those in a garden or the sea. Check the specified conditions, evidence for the claimed disposal route, and whether a suitable local facility accepts the material.
