Lesson 1: Definition and Brief History of Organic Chemistry

What do sugar, cooking gas, and a plastic container have in common? Their chemistry involves carbon compounds. But does every compound containing carbon belong to organic chemistry?

1. What is organic chemistry?

Organic chemistry studies the structures, properties, preparation, and reactions of organic compounds. These are carbon compounds, with certain carbon-containing substances traditionally studied as inorganic compounds.

Many organic compounds contain carbon–hydrogen (C–H) bonds. They may also contain oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, or other elements. However, a C–H bond is common, not required.

Two shortcuts to avoid
  • “It contains carbon, so it must be organic.” Carbon dioxide and carbonates are familiar exceptions.
  • “It comes from nature, so it must be organic.” Water is natural, but it is an inorganic compound.

Everyday connections

Food and fuel

Sucrose in table sugar is an organic compound. Propane and butane, common components of LPG, are also organic compounds.

Medicines and materials

Paracetamol is an organic compound. Polyethylene, used in many plastic bags and containers, is an organic polymer—a substance made of long chains of repeating units.

Classify the named substance, not an entire product. Foods, medicines, and household products may be mixtures of organic and inorganic substances.

A useful starting guide

  1. Read the formula. Is carbon, represented by C, present? If no carbon is present, the compound is inorganic.
  2. Check familiar exceptions. Carbon monoxide (CO), carbon dioxide (CO2), carbonates, and hydrogen carbonates are conventionally inorganic.
  3. Use what you know about the compound. Do not decide from its source or the presence of C–H bonds alone. A formula is a starting clue, not a universal classification test.
Worked example 1 · Use the formula

Methane, CH4

Observe: It contains carbon bonded to hydrogen.

Interpret: Methane belongs to the hydrocarbons, compounds containing only carbon and hydrogen.

Conclude: Methane is organic. Its classification does not depend on where it was obtained.

Worked example 2 · Check the exception

Carbon dioxide, CO2

Observe: Carbon is present.

Interpret: Carbon dioxide is a simple oxide of carbon, conventionally classified as inorganic.

Conclude: CO2 is inorganic, even when released by a living organism during respiration.

Worked example 3 · Look at the bonds

Urea, CO(NH2)2

Observe: Urea contains carbon and hydrogen, but no C–H bonds.

Conclude: Urea is still organic. Therefore, requiring every organic compound to have a C–H bond would incorrectly exclude urea.

2. How did the meaning of “organic” change?

The earlier belief: vitalism

Early chemists distinguished substances obtained from living organisms from those obtained from minerals. One influential belief was that making organic substances required a special “vital force” found only in living things.

In this context, vitalism predicted that an organic substance could not be prepared from inorganic starting materials without the involvement of a living organism.

The evidence: Wöhler’s urea synthesis in 1828

Friedrich Wöhler prepared urea from ammonium cyanate, an inorganic salt. Urea was already known as a substance found in urine, but his preparation did not require a living organism.

Read the reaction in three steps

  1. Identify the starting compound: ammonium cyanate, shown on the left.
  2. Identify the product: urea, shown on the right.
  3. Connect the result to the claim: an organic product formed without a living organism, challenging the claim that a vital force was required.
Historical accuracy: Wöhler’s experiment was an important challenge to vitalism. It did not immediately end the belief or establish the whole field by itself; acceptance changed as further evidence accumulated.

3. From identifying substances to understanding structures

  1. Early study · Where did it come from?
    Substances were grouped largely by their sources, such as organisms or minerals.
  2. 1828 · Can it be made in a laboratory?
    Wöhler’s urea synthesis became an important example of preparing an organic substance from an inorganic starting material.
  3. 19th century · How are the atoms connected?
    Structural theory developed through contributions from chemists including Kekulé, Couper, and Butlerov. Chemists increasingly connected a substance’s behavior with the arrangement of its atoms.
  4. 20th century to today · How can we identify and design molecules?
    Improved synthesis and methods such as spectroscopy expanded the study of molecular structure and reactions, supporting work in medicine, biology, and materials science.

Why structure matters: ethanol and dimethyl ether

Both have the molecular formula C2H6O, but their atoms connect differently:

Ethanol

CH3–CH2–OH

Oxygen is bonded to a carbon and a hydrogen.

Dimethyl ether

CH3–O–CH3

Oxygen is bonded to two carbons.

Meaning: A molecular formula tells us the number of each kind of atom. A structural formula also shows connections. Different connections can produce different properties.

You do not need to name these structures yet. This distinction prepares you for later lessons on structures and nomenclature.

Guided drill 4 · Correct the explanation

A student says, “Ethanol and dimethyl ether must be the same substance because both are C2H6O.” Identify what is correct in the statement and what needs correcting.

Show explanationHide explanation

The atom counts are the same. However, the connections differ: ethanol has an O–H bond, while dimethyl ether has oxygen between two carbons. They are different substances. Atom counts alone do not specify a unique structure.

9. Why was producing urea from ammonium cyanate stronger evidence against vitalism than simply extracting urea from urine?

Show explanationHide explanation

Extraction only separates urea already produced by an organism, so it is consistent with vitalism. Preparing urea from an inorganic starting compound without an organism challenges the proposed need for a vital force.

5. What to remember

  • Scope: Organic chemistry examines organic compounds—their structures, properties, preparation, and reactions.
  • Classification: Organic compounds contain carbon, but certain carbon compounds are conventionally inorganic. C–H bonds are common, not essential.
  • Source: Organic compounds can occur naturally or be prepared in a laboratory or factory.
  • Historical evidence: Wöhler prepared urea from ammonium cyanate in 1828, challenging the need for a vital force.
  • Structure: Knowing which atoms are present is not enough; their connections also matter.
