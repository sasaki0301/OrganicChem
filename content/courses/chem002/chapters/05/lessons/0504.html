Cycloalkenes: Structure and Naming

Learn to read a ring containing a double bond, choose its numbering, and name its substituents. Compare structural and skeletal formulas as you work.

The basicsNaming stepsWorked examplesPractice

1. Meet the cycloalkenes

A cycloalkene is a cyclic hydrocarbon with a carbon–carbon double bond. A ring with six carbons and one C=C bond is cyclohexene.

In the structural formula, CH₂ means a carbon bonded to two hydrogens; CH means a carbon bonded to one. In the skeletal formula, every unlabeled corner is a carbon, and hydrogens attached to carbon are omitted. The two lines mark a double bond.

Formula shortcut: For a hydrocarbon with one ring, one C=C within that ring, and no other rings or multiple bonds, the molecular formula is CnH2n−2. Cyclohexene has six carbons, so its formula is C₆H₁₀. Additional rings or multiple bonds change the formula.

The two C=C carbons are described as sp²-hybridized; their double bond consists of one σ and one π bond. Rotation around C=C is restricted. This alone does not guarantee a cis–trans or E/Z pair: substituents and ring geometry also matter. Our drawings show connectivity, not the three-dimensional shape or unspecified stereochemistry.

2. A reliable naming method

For the simple alkyl-substituted cycloalkenes in this lesson, use this sequence:

  1. Choose the ring. Count its carbons and use the root with cyclo- and -ene.
  2. Number the C=C. Its carbons must be 1 and 2. Try each end of the double bond as carbon 1, then continue around the ring.
  3. Compare substituent locants. Sort each possible set and choose the lower number at the first point of difference. A smaller sum is not the rule.
  4. Build the name. Give each substituent its locant, use di-, tri-, etc. for repeats, and list different substituents alphabetically. If complete locant sets tie, give the alphabetically first substituent the lower locant.

For one double bond in a ring, its location is understood: cyclohexene and cyclohex-1-ene identify the same parent. We use the explicit -1-ene form in the worked names.

These steps apply to the simple examples shown. More complex molecules with functional groups, multiple double bonds, or exocyclic double bonds need additional parent-selection and numbering rules.

3. Worked examples

Example 1 · A substituent on the double bond

  1. Count five ring carbons: cyclopentene.
  2. Both ends of C=C can start numbering. Putting the methyl-bearing carbon at C-1 gives methyl locant 1 instead of 2.
  3. Name: 1-methylcyclopent-1-ene (also 1-methylcyclopentene).

Example 2 · A substituent away from C=C

  1. The six-carbon ring is cyclohex-1-ene.
  2. Number C=C as 1 and 2. The ethyl-bearing carbon becomes C-3 in this direction; the other direction would give C-6.
  3. Name: 3-ethylcyclohex-1-ene.

The two-segment side chain is ethyl. The branch is outside the parent ring.

Example 3 · Compare the whole locant set

One direction places methyl at C-1 and ethyl at C-5: sorted locants 1,5. Reversing the direction gives methyl at C-2 and ethyl at C-4: 2,4. At the first difference, 1 is lower than 2, so choose 1,5.

Write the prefixes in alphabetical order: ethyl before methyl. The name is 5-ethyl-1-methylcyclohex-1-ene. Alphabetical order controls the written order here; it does not override the lower locant set.

See the alternative numbering

This direction gives 2-methyl and 4-ethyl, or 2,4. It loses to 1,5 at the first difference.

5. Key takeaways

  • Cycloalkenes contain a ring and a C=C bond.
  • For one ring with one endocyclic double bond and no additional unsaturation, the formula is CnH2n−2.
  • In the simple examples here, number the C=C carbons 1 and 2; choose the lowest substituent locant set at the first difference.
  • Use alphabetical order to write different substituents and, when the complete locant sets tie, to break the numbering tie.
  • Structural and skeletal formulas show the same connections; unmarked stereochemistry remains unspecified.
