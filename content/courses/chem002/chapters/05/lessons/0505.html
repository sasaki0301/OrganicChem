Alkynes and Naming Alkynes

Recognize a carbon–carbon triple bond, choose and number the parent chain, and build an alkyne name. Use the structural, condensed and skeletal formulas together as you practice.

1. What makes an alkyne?

An alkyne is a hydrocarbon containing at least one carbon–carbon triple bond, written C≡C. It is unsaturated. The simplest is ethyne, H–C≡C–H, also called acetylene.

  • One C≡C contains one σ bond and two π bonds.
  • Each carbon in an ordinary acyclic triple bond is sp-hybridized. The attached atoms are arranged approximately 180° apart, giving a linear region at C≡C.
  • In skeletal drawings, the triple bond is shown by three lines. Each unlabeled line end or corner is a carbon; hydrogens attached to carbon are omitted.
Formula shortcut: An acyclic hydrocarbon with exactly one C≡C and no other multiple bonds has the formula CnH2n−2. For but-1-yne, n = 4 and the formula is C₄H₆. This shortcut does not apply unchanged when additional rings or multiple bonds are present.

Alkynes can undergo addition reactions: atoms or groups add across C≡C. The outcome depends on the reagents and conditions; this lesson concentrates on naming.

2. A step-by-step naming method

Use these steps for the acyclic hydrocarbons in this lesson, which have one triple bond and no double bonds:

  1. Choose the parent: Find the longest continuous carbon chain that includes both carbons of C≡C. Follow the chain around corners; a line pointing sideways can belong to the parent.
  2. Number the chain: Start at the end that gives the triple bond the lower locant. Its locant is the lower of the two numbers on the C≡C carbons.
  3. Name the parent: Use the carbon-count root and -yne, placing the locant before the suffix: but-1-yne.
  4. Add substituents: Give each branch its number. For repeated identical branches use di-, tri-, and so on; list different substituent names alphabetically. If the triple bond has the same locant from either end, compare the substituent locants at the first difference.
The triple bond takes priority over branch locants in these examples. More complex structures, such as enynes, polyynes, and compounds with higher-priority functional groups, require additional rules.

Older names such as 1-butyne identify the same compound as but-1-yne. This lesson uses the form with the locant immediately before -yne.

3. Worked examples

Example 1 · A terminal triple bond

Condensed: HC≡C–CH₂–CH₃

  1. The longest chain containing C≡C has four carbons: but-.
  2. Number from the terminal alkyne end; the triple bond starts at C-1, not C-3.
  3. Name: but-1-yne.

The C≡C bond reaches the end of the carbon chain, so this is a terminal alkyne. A terminal alkyne has a hydrogen attached to a triple-bond carbon.

Example 2 · An internal triple bond

Condensed: CH₃–C≡C–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃

  1. There are six carbons in the parent chain.
  2. Compare directions: the triple bond begins at C-2 from the left, or C-4 from the right.
  3. Use the lower locant: hex-2-yne.

There is a carbon group attached at each end of the triple bond, so this is an internal alkyne.

Example 3 · The parent chain turns a corner

Condensed: HC≡C–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₃

  1. Start at the terminal C≡C and trace the longest path through both triple-bond carbons. At the junction, the upper path continues through two carbons; the lower path reaches one carbon.
  2. The parent therefore contains six carbons, even though its last two segments turn upward. The remaining one-carbon line is methyl at C-4.
  3. The triple bond is at C-1. The name is 4-methylhex-1-yne.

The appearance of a branch does not decide whether a line belongs to the parent. Follow connected carbons and compare lengths.

Example 4 · A second turning chain and two substituents

Condensed: CH₃–C≡C–CH(CH₂CH₃)–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₃

  1. Trace the longest chain through C≡C: it has seven carbons and turns upward at C-4. The lower two-carbon path is an ethyl substituent.
  2. Number from the left: the triple bond begins at C-2; from the other end it begins at C-5.
  3. Identify ethyl at C-4 and methyl at C-5. Alphabetize: 4-ethyl-5-methylhept-2-yne.

Challenge · Explain your numbering

In 4-methylpent-1-yne, why don’t we number from the end closer to the methyl group?

Show answer
Numbering from the triple-bond end gives the triple bond locant 1; numbering from the other end gives it locant 4. For this simple alkyne, the triple-bond locant is considered before the substituent locant.

5. Key takeaways

  • Alkynes contain C≡C, comprising one σ and two π bonds; the triple-bond region is linear in the acyclic examples shown.
  • For one acyclic triple bond and no other multiple bonds, use CnH2n−2.
  • Choose the longest continuous chain containing C≡C, even when the skeletal path turns a corner.
  • Give the triple bond the lower locant, then name and locate the substituents.
  • The condensed, structural, and skeletal formulas represent the same connectivity.
