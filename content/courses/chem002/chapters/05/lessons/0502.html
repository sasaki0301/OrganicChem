Cis–Trans and E–Z Isomerism in Alkenes

Decide whether a double bond has distinct geometries, then describe the arrangement using matching groups or CIP priorities.

1. Why can a C=C have different arrangements?

A C=C contains a σ bond and a π bond. Rotation that interchanges the groups around the double bond requires substantial disruption of π overlap, so rotation is strongly restricted. Ordinary single-bond rotation can also have barriers; it is often much easier than rotation about C=C.

For the simple acyclic alkenes in this lesson, first check each end of C=C: each double-bond carbon must have two different substituents. If one end has two identical groups, exchanging them produces no distinct E/Z pair.

No E/Z pair: propene

One double-bond carbon has two H atoms. Neither E nor Z applies. The drawings show the same connectivity; the skeletal diagram includes H labels here to make the comparison explicit.

Checkpoint

Can CH₂=C(CH₃)₂ have an E/Z pair?

Show answer
No. One end has two H atoms and the other has two identical CH₃ groups, so neither end has two different substituents.

2. Cis and trans: compare matching groups

In simple examples with one comparable pair of identical groups across C=C, cis means that pair is on the same side of the double bond; trans means opposite sides. Each end must still have two different substituents for distinct geometries.

cis-but-2-ene

Compare the two CH₃ groups: both are above the double-bond axis. They are on the same side → cis. Here CH₃ has higher priority than H on each end, so this example is also (Z)-but-2-ene.

trans-but-2-ene

The two CH₃ groups lie on opposite sides → trans. Since CH₃ outranks H at each end, this example is also (E)-but-2-ene.

In the skeletal views, unlabelled line ends are carbon atoms. H is shown explicitly here where needed to emphasize the groups being compared. These are distinct spatial arrangements with the same atom connectivity.

3. Assign CIP priority on each end

E/Z uses Cahn–Ingold–Prelog (CIP) priority independently at each double-bond carbon. Start at each attached atom, moving outward only when the comparison is tied.

  1. Compare directly attached atoms. Higher atomic number wins: Br (35) > Cl (17) > O (8) > C (6) > H (1).
  2. If the first atoms tie, compare the next layer. List atoms attached to each tied atom, excluding the C=C carbon, in decreasing atomic number; compare at the first difference. Continue outward if needed.
  3. Multiple bonds: for CIP comparison, use duplicate-atom conventions; a double-bonded atom is treated as attached to duplicate representations of its partner. This is a ranking device, not extra physical atoms.

Worked comparison · Ethyl versus methyl

Both groups attach through carbon. For –CH₂CH₃, the directly attached carbon has the comparison list [C, H, H]. For –CH₃ it has [H, H, H]. At the first difference C > H, so ethyl ranks higher.

Worked comparison · A multiple bond

Compare –CH=CH₂ with –CH₂CH₃. The first atoms are both carbon. Applying the duplicate-atom convention gives the first comparison lists [C, C, H] and [C, H, H], respectively. At the second entry C > H, so –CH=CH₂ ranks higher.

These examples use common initial CIP comparisons; more complex structures can require additional sequence rules.

4. E or Z: compare the higher-priority groups

  1. Verify that each C=C carbon has two different substituents.
  2. Rank the two substituents on the left; then rank the two on the right independently.
  3. Z (zusammen, together): the higher-priority groups are on the same side. E (entgegen, opposite): they are on opposite sides.
  4. Add the descriptor in parentheses before the name when the configuration is known: (Z)-but-2-ene or (E)-but-2-ene.

Worked example · Four different substituents

At the left C=C carbon, Cl outranks H. At the right, Br outranks the C of CH₃. Cl and Br are both above the axis: Z. The priority comparison is made separately on each end.

When cis and Z are not equivalent

The two CH₃ groups are on the same side, so their relationship is cis. For E/Z, Cl outranks CH₃ at the left carbon; CH₃ outranks H at the right. Cl is below while that CH₃ is above, so the higher-priority groups are opposite: E.

Thus, for this specified matching-methyl comparison, the structure is cis with respect to the methyl groups and has E configuration. Do not assume every cis label corresponds to Z.

E/Z also works for eligible simple alkenes such as but-2-ene; it is not reserved for molecules with four different substituents. If either double-bond carbon has two identical substituents, no E/Z descriptor is assigned.

Practice 5 · Compare systems

In the example above where both methyl groups are on the same side but Cl and the other methyl have higher priority, why are the descriptors cis and E?

Show answer
Cis compares the matching methyl groups, which are together. E compares the higher-priority groups, Cl and the methyl on the other end, which are opposite. The two systems compare different groups.

Challenge · Multiple bonds in a priority comparison

Which ranks higher: –CH=CH₂ or –CH₂CH₃? Explain the first difference.

Show answer
–CH=CH₂. After the tied first carbon, the comparison lists are [C, C, H] and [C, H, H]; the second entry C outranks H.

6. Key takeaways

  • Each C=C carbon needs two different substituents for a simple E/Z pair.
  • Use cis/trans for suitable matching groups; E/Z compares CIP priorities independently at each end.
  • Compare directly attached atoms first, then move outward through tied layers.
  • Z means the higher-priority groups are together; E means opposite.
  • An unspecified condensed formula does not by itself identify a particular geometry.
