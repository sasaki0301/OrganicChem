1. Quick reference

Each formula below applies only to the hydrocarbon described in its row, with no additional rings or multiple bonds. A substituent may change the total carbon count, but not the stated degree of unsaturation.

2. Alkenes and naming

An alkene has a C=C double bond containing one σ and one π bond. In the simple examples here, choose the longest chain containing the double bond, then number from the end giving that bond the lower locant before locating substituents.

Worked review · Follow the parent

Condensed: CH₂=CH–CH(CH₃)–CH₃

The longest chain containing C=C has four carbons. Number from the double-bond end: C=C begins at C-1 and methyl is at C-3. Name: 3-methylbut-1-ene.

3. Cis–trans and E/Z isomerism

The π bond restricts rotation about C=C. For an ordinary acyclic alkene to have E/Z stereoisomers, each double-bond carbon must have two different substituents. The four substituents need not all be different.

  1. Assign priority separately at each double-bond carbon using CIP rules: higher atomic number at the first point of difference receives higher priority.
  2. Compare the higher-priority group on each side: same side is Z; opposite sides is E.

Worked review · But-2-ene

Each C=C carbon has CH₃ and H. Carbon has a higher atomic number than hydrogen, so each CH₃ has higher priority. In the drawing, both methyl groups are on the same side: (Z)-but-2-ene, also called cis-but-2-ene.

Cis/trans compares an appropriate pair of groups; E/Z compares the higher-priority group at each carbon. They need not correspond in every molecule. A condensed formula such as CH₃CH=CHCH₃ does not by itself say which stereoisomer is present. Restricted rotation alone does not guarantee that a distinct E/Z pair exists.

4. Cycloalkenes

A cycloalkene has a C=C in a carbon ring. For the simple monocyclic examples with one endocyclic double bond, assign its carbons 1 and 2, then choose the numbering direction with the lowest substituent locants.

Worked review · Number the ring

Condensed: cyclo-[CH=CH–CH(CH₂CH₃)–(CH₂)₃], with the last CH₂ connected back to the first CH.

The ring contains six carbons. The ethyl group gets locant 3 in the direction shown, rather than 6 in the reverse direction: 3-ethylcyclohex-1-ene (also 3-ethylcyclohexene). The ring drawing shows connectivity, not its exact 3D shape or stereochemistry at the substituted carbon.

5. Alkynes and naming

An alkyne contains C≡C: one σ and two π bonds. In ordinary acyclic alkynes, the triple-bonded carbons are described as sp-hybridized and the attached atoms lie approximately along a straight line. The triple bond itself does not have cis/trans or E/Z isomerism.

Worked review · Count through the corner

Condensed: HC≡C–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₃

The six-carbon parent containing C≡C turns toward the two-carbon path. Number from the triple-bond end: the triple bond starts at C-1, and the remaining methyl branch is at C-4. Name: 4-methylhex-1-yne.

6. Cycloalkynes

A cycloalkyne has C≡C within a ring. The ring bends the alkyne framework away from its preferred near-linear geometry, contributing to strain. Unsubstituted cyclooctyne is commonly isolable but remains strained; structure and conditions influence stability.

Worked review · Ring name and formula

Condensed: cyclo-[C≡C–(CH₂)₆], with the last CH₂ connected back to the first C.

The ring has eight carbons and one endocyclic triple bond: cyclooctyne or cyclooct-1-yne. With exactly one ring and one triple bond, its formula is C₈H₁₂.

Some suitably designed cyclooctyne reagents react with azides in copper-free click chemistry. Rates and suitability depend on the reagents and conditions.

7. Compounds with both C=C and C≡C

For the simple acyclic enynes here, choose the longest continuous chain containing both bonds. Compare the sorted multiple-bond locant sets from both ends. If the sets tie, assign the lower locant to C=C. Write en before yne in the name.

Worked review · Numbering tie

Condensed: CH₂=CH–CH₂–C≡CH

From either end the sorted set is 1,4. The tie-break gives C=C locant 1, so the name is pent-1-en-4-yne.

If one direction gives the lower set, it wins even if C≡C has the lower locant. For instance, 1,3 beats 2,4; the double-bond tie-break applies only when the overall sets tie. Complex structures may need other parent-selection rules, and not every molecule has a single chain containing all multiple bonds.
