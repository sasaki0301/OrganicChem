1. Recognizing an alcohol

An alcohol has a hydroxyl group, –OH, attached to a saturated carbon atom. In CH₃CH₂OH, the OH-bearing carbon is at the end of the two-carbon chain.

Not every compound with an OH group is an alcohol. For example, phenols have OH directly attached to an aromatic ring; carboxylic acids contain a –COOH group.

2. Classify the OH-bearing carbon

Count carbon atoms directly attached to the carbon bearing OH: one means primary, two means secondary, and three means tertiary.

Worked review · Propan-2-ol

Condensed: CH₃CH(OH)CH₃

The OH-bearing carbon is directly attached to two carbon atoms, so this alcohol is secondary.

Methanol is a special case: its OH-bearing carbon has zero carbon neighbours. It is commonly grouped with primary alcohols in introductory discussions, although it does not literally have one carbon neighbour.

3. Naming simple alcohols

For the saturated alcohols in this chapter, where OH is the principal functional group:

  1. Choose the longest continuous carbon chain containing the OH-bearing carbon.
  2. Number from the end nearer the OH group.
  3. Replace the final -e in the parent alkane name with -ol; include the OH locant when needed.
  4. Number and name substituents, alphabetizing different simple substituents.

Worked review · A branch near OH

Condensed: CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂OH

The longest chain containing OH has three carbons. Number from the OH end: OH is on C-1 and methyl on C-2. The name is 2-methylpropan-1-ol.

Worked review · Trace around a corner

Condensed: CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃

The longer route through the junction gives six carbons and contains OH. Number from the left: OH is at C-2 and the remaining methyl group is at C-4. The name is 4-methylhexan-2-ol.

In a skeletal formula, unlabeled corners and ends represent carbon atoms; OH is shown explicitly. Drawings without stereochemical markings specify connectivity, not the configuration at stereogenic carbons.

4. Properties: connect cause and effect

PropertyWhat to remember
PolarityOxygen attracts shared electrons strongly; the OH region is polar, while the carbon–hydrogen portion is comparatively nonpolar.
Hydrogen bondingAlcohols can donate and accept hydrogen bonds with one another and with water.
Boiling pointAlcohols often boil at higher temperatures than comparable hydrocarbons, partly because of intermolecular hydrogen bonding.
Water solubilityFor similar straight-chain alcohols with one OH group, solubility generally decreases as the hydrocarbon chain lengthens. Structure and temperature matter.
AciditySimple alcohols are very weak acids, broadly comparable to water. Exact acidity depends on structure and solvent.

Worked review · Ethanol and pentan-1-ol

Ethanol: CH₃CH₂OH   Pentan-1-ol: CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂OH

Both can hydrogen-bond with water, but pentan-1-ol has a larger hydrocarbon portion and is much less soluble. Ethanol is miscible with water, meaning they mix in all proportions.

Worked review · Conjugate base

CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃CH₂O⁻ + H⁺

When ethanol donates H⁺ from its OH group, the conjugate base is ethoxide, CH₃CH₂O⁻. In water, dissociation is very limited.

6. Key terms

  • Hydroxyl: the –OH group.
  • Primary / secondary / tertiary: labels based on the number of carbon neighbours of the OH-bearing carbon.
  • Hydrogen bond: an attraction involving a suitable donor hydrogen and an electron-pair acceptor.
  • Miscible: two liquids mix in all proportions.
  • Alkoxide: the conjugate base RO⁻ produced when an alcohol loses H⁺.
