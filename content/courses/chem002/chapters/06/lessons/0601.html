Alcohols and Naming Alcohols

Recognize the hydroxyl group, classify the carbon that bears it, and name simple alcohols from structural, condensed and skeletal formulas.

IdentifyClassifyNaming stepsWorked examplesPractice

1. What is an alcohol?

An alcohol has a hydroxyl group, –OH, attached to a saturated carbon atom. The oxygen is bonded to that carbon and to hydrogen. For example, ethanol has condensed formula CH₃CH₂OH.

In the structural formula, carbon groups are shown. In the skeletal formula, unlabeled corners and ends are carbon atoms and their hydrogens are implied; O and its H are shown.

Not every –OH-containing compound is classified as an alcohol. Phenol has –OH directly attached to an aromatic ring, and a carboxylic acid contains –COOH. An –OH attached directly to a C=C carbon is an enol. This introductory lesson focuses on simple alcohols.

2. Primary, secondary and tertiary

Find the carbon attached to –OH, then count how many other carbon atoms are directly attached to it. Count carbons, not hydrogens or total atoms.

ClassCarbon neighboursExample
Primary (1°)OneCH₃CH₂OH: ethanol
Secondary (2°)TwoCH₃CH(OH)CH₃: propan-2-ol
Tertiary (3°)Three(CH₃)₃COH: 2-methylpropan-2-ol

Methanol, CH₃OH, is a special case: its –OH-bearing carbon has zero carbon neighbours. It is commonly grouped with primary alcohols in elementary discussions and reactions, but does not literally meet the one-neighbour definition.

Worked classification · Propan-2-ol

Condensed: CH₃CH(OH)CH₃

The carbon bonded to OH is connected directly to two other carbons. Therefore, propan-2-ol is secondary.

3. A naming method

For the simple saturated monohydric alcohols in this lesson, where alcohol is the principal functional group:

  1. Find the parent chain: select the longest continuous carbon chain containing the carbon bonded to –OH. Trace around corners.
  2. Number from the end nearer –OH: the carbon bearing it gets the lowest possible locant before ordinary alkyl branches.
  3. Name the parent: replace the final -e of the alkane with -ol. Put the hydroxyl locant before -ol: butan-2-ol.
  4. Add substituents: give each its locant, use di-, tri- for repeats, and list different simple substituents alphabetically.

Methanol and ethanol need no hydroxyl locant because the position is unambiguous. Terminal alcohols with longer parents may still need a locant: propan-1-ol.

Compounds with several –OH groups, multiple bonds or higher-priority functional groups need additional rules. In simple cycloalkanols, the ring carbon bearing OH is C-1; number the ring to give substituents the lowest locants.

4. Worked naming examples

Example 1 · An OH-bearing end

Condensed: CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂OH

  1. The longest chain containing the OH-bearing carbon has three carbons.
  2. Start at the OH end: OH at C-1; methyl at C-2.
  3. Name: 2-methylpropan-1-ol. The OH carbon has one carbon neighbour, so it is primary.

Example 2 · Follow the parent around the junction

Condensed: CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃

  1. At the junction, the upper path has two carbons; the lower path has one. Following the longer path gives a six-carbon chain containing OH.
  2. Number from the left: OH is at C-2 and the remaining methyl branch is at C-4.
  3. Name: 4-methylhexan-2-ol. The OH-bearing carbon has two carbon neighbours: secondary.

Example 3 · An ethyl substituent

Condensed: CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH(CH₂CH₃)CH₂CH₃

  1. The longest path containing OH has six carbons; either equivalent two-carbon path at the junction can be included.
  2. Number from the nearer end: OH at C-3 and the remaining ethyl at C-4.
  3. Name: 4-ethylhexan-3-ol.

The formulas in these examples specify connectivity; configurations at stereogenic carbons, where applicable, are not specified.

5. Connecting structure to properties

The O–H and C–O bonds are polar. Alcohol molecules can hydrogen-bond with one another, often giving them higher boiling points than comparable hydrocarbons. Ethanol boils at a higher temperature than propane. Water solubility depends on the whole structure: as a hydrocarbon portion becomes larger, it can reduce solubility despite an OH group.

7. Key takeaways

  • Simple alcohols contain –OH attached to a saturated carbon.
  • Classify by counting carbon neighbours of the carbon bearing OH.
  • Choose the longest eligible chain containing the OH-bearing carbon and number OH as low as possible.
  • Use the -ol suffix and identify the positions of OH and substituents.
  • Alcohols can form hydrogen bonds; molecular structure affects physical properties.
