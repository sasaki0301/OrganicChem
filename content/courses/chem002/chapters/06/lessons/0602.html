Properties of Alcohols

Connect the polar hydroxyl group and the carbon chain to intermolecular forces, boiling point, water solubility, and weak acidity.

1. One molecule, different regions

In an alcohol such as ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH, oxygen attracts shared electrons more strongly than carbon and hydrogen. The C–O and O–H bonds are polar; oxygen has partial negative character (δ−), and the O–H hydrogen has partial positive character (δ+).

Worked example · Ethanol

CH₃–CH₂–Oδ−–Hδ+

The OH region can interact strongly with water. The carbon–hydrogen portion is comparatively nonpolar. Overall behavior depends on the entire molecule, not only the OH group.

Partial charges describe unequal electron distribution. They do not mean that an ordinary alcohol molecule contains a separate O²⁻ ion or H⁺ ion.

2. Hydrogen bonding

A hydrogen bond is an attraction between a hydrogen covalently bonded to an electronegative atom such as O and a suitable electron-pair-bearing atom such as O on another molecule. Alcohols can both donate a hydrogen bond through O–H and accept one at oxygen.

Alcohol molecules can hydrogen-bond with other alcohol molecules and with water. Alkanes lack the usual O–H, N–H or F–H hydrogen-bond donor bonds. All molecules also experience dispersion forces; hydrogen bonding is a particularly important attraction in alcohols.

3. Boiling points: comparing like with like

Boiling occurs when a liquid’s vapor pressure equals the external pressure. Separating molecules into a gas requires energy to overcome intermolecular attractions; ordinary boiling does not break the covalent O–H bond.

Worked comparison · Same molecular formula

CompoundCondensed structureApproximate normal boiling point
EthanolCH₃CH₂OH78 °C
Dimethyl etherCH₃OCH₃−25 °C

Both have formula C₂H₆O, but ethanol has an O–H bond and can donate hydrogen bonds to other ethanol molecules. Dimethyl ether has no O–H bond, so pure ether molecules cannot donate such bonds to one another. Their different attractions help explain the large boiling-point difference.

Normal boiling points are approximately at 1 atm; values are rounded. Dimethyl ether can accept hydrogen bonds from suitable donors such as water.

Among similar straight-chain alcohols with one OH group, boiling point generally increases as the carbon chain grows, because dispersion forces become stronger. Branching and molecular shape also influence boiling point, so compare specific structures rather than using chain length alone as a universal rule.

4. Water solubility: two competing features

The OH group can hydrogen-bond with water. The hydrocarbon portion interacts less favorably with water than the OH region. For similar straight-chain alcohols with one OH, water solubility generally decreases as the carbon chain gets longer. Temperature, branching, and the number of OH groups also matter.

Worked comparison · Ethanol and pentan-1-ol

Ethanol: CH₃CH₂OH    Pentan-1-ol: CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂OH

Both have one OH group that can interact with water. Pentan-1-ol has a longer hydrocarbon portion, so it is much less soluble in water. Ethanol is miscible with water: the liquids mix in all proportions.

Soluble means some amount can dissolve; miscible means two liquids mix in all proportions. “Less soluble” does not mean “unable to dissolve at all.”

5. Alcohols as weak acids

An alcohol can donate the hydrogen from its O–H group. The conjugate base is an alkoxide, RO⁻:

ROH + H₂O ⇌ RO⁻ + H₃O⁺

In water this equilibrium strongly favors the left for simple alcohols: only a very small fraction donates a proton to water. Simple alcohols are very weak acids, broadly comparable to water in acidity; their exact acidities depend on structure and solvent. Alcohol oxygen can also accept a proton in suitable acid–base reactions.

Checkpoint · Find the conjugate base of ethanol
Removing H⁺ from the O–H group of CH₃CH₂OH gives CH₃CH₂O⁻, the ethoxide ion.

Show an example answer
The OH group can hydrogen-bond with water, but the long hydrocarbon portion also affects the mixing. For similar straight-chain alcohols with one OH group, increasing chain length generally lowers water solubility. An OH group alone does not guarantee miscibility.

Practice 7 · Distinguish bond types

During boiling, are covalent bonds within each ethanol molecule normally broken?

Show answer
No. Boiling separates molecules into the gas phase by overcoming intermolecular attractions. The ordinary covalent bonds within each ethanol molecule remain intact.

7. Key terms and takeaways

  • Partial charge: an uneven distribution of electron density, indicated by δ+ or δ−.
  • Hydrogen bond: an intermolecular attraction involving a suitable O–H, N–H or F–H donor and an electron-pair acceptor.
  • Normal boiling point: boiling temperature at an external pressure of 1 atm.
  • Miscible: able to mix with another liquid in all proportions.
  • Alkoxide: the conjugate base RO⁻ formed when an alcohol loses H⁺.
